# Deliberation 01: motivating derived manifolds

## Status and verdict

This dossier concerns the material provisionally assigned to Talk 1 in
`program.tex`. The title and chapter boundary remain provisional. Each seminar
talk lasts 90 minutes.

The proposed unit is viable as a full talk, provided it follows one coherent
spine and does not become a miniature survey. The recommended spine is:

> Transverse intersections have the expected tangent spaces; non-transverse
> intersections retain a two-term deformation-obstruction complex; complexes,
> unlike kernels, behave well in families; elliptic moduli problems locally
> lead to exactly this structure.

The talk should contain about 70--75 minutes of planned exposition. The
remaining time is for questions, recaps, and board-time variation. There is
already enough material. Adding a second application or a definition of
derived manifolds would make the talk less effective.

Pardon's completed 2024 proceedings article supplies the complementary
motivation used here. The mathematical claims developed in the talk are
elementary differential topology and linear algebra; the elliptic
finite-dimensional reduction is explicitly quoted rather than proved. The
unfinished July 2026 manuscript is not used in Talk 1.

## Role in the seminar

The first unit should make the central problem visible before introducing
$C^\infty$-rings or higher-categorical machinery:

> A non-transverse zero locus has deformation and obstruction information that
> its underlying set does not remember.

The unit succeeds if a participant leaves wanting an object which remembers the
equations, is stable under pullback, and behaves well in families. It should not
yet answer what that object is.

Later units may assume the transverse fiber product theorem, the two worked
examples below, and the four final takeaways. They may not assume a definition
or universal property of derived manifolds.

## Audience contract

1. Assume smooth manifolds, tangent spaces, derivatives, and the regular value
   theorem.
2. Recall transversality rather than treating it as prior shared vocabulary.
3. Prove or carefully derive the tangent-space statement for a transverse
   fiber product.
4. Introduce the two-term complex as a preview and a useful calculation, not as
   a definition of a derived manifold.
5. Use no $\infty$-categorical formalism. The phrase “homotopy pullback” may be
   named once as the later formal answer.
6. State finite-dimensional reduction as a quoted theorem schema and explain
   every analytic term needed to parse it. Do not attempt its proof.

## The elementary engine

Let $f\colon X\to Z$ and $g\colon Y\to Z$ be smooth maps. They are
*transverse* if, for every $(x,y)$ with $f(x)=g(y)=z$,
\[
  \operatorname{im}(D_xf)+\operatorname{im}(D_yg)=T_zZ.
\]
Equivalently, the map
\[
  D_xf-D_yg\colon T_xX\oplus T_yY\longrightarrow T_zZ
\]
is surjective.

The principal elementary result should be the following.

> **Transverse fiber product theorem.** If $f$ and $g$ are transverse, then
> $X\times_ZY$ is a smooth manifold of dimension
> $\dim X+\dim Y-\dim Z$, and
> \[
>   T_{(x,y)}(X\times_ZY)
>   \cong
>   \ker\bigl(D_xf-D_yg\bigr).
> \]

This can be proved from the regular value theorem, either directly in local
coordinates or by observing that $f\times g\colon X\times Y\to Z\times Z$ is
transverse to the diagonal. The diagonal proof is conceptually cleaner; a
brief coordinate explanation should still be supplied for participants who do
not immediately see the reduction.

The important feature is not only the existence of the fiber product. The
theorem identifies its tangent space as the kernel of a surjective map. Once
surjectivity fails, the same linear map still exists, but its cokernel is no
longer zero. This is the entrance point for obstruction theory.

## First worked example: a tangent intersection

Let $A$ be the $x$-axis and $B$ the parabola in $\mathbb R^2$, parametrized by
\[
  i(s)=(s,0),
  \qquad
  j(t)=(t,t^2).
\]
Their ordinary intersection is the single point $(0,0)$. The linearized
fiber-product complex at that point is
\[
  \bigl[T_0A\oplus T_0B
  \xrightarrow{\,D_0i-D_0j\,}
  T_0\mathbb R^2\bigr]
  =
  \bigl[\mathbb R^2\xrightarrow{(u,v)\mapsto(u-v,0)}\mathbb R^2\bigr],
\]
placed in cohomological degrees $0$ and $1$. Hence
\[
  H^0\cong\mathbb R,
  \qquad
  H^1\cong\mathbb R.
\]
The first group is the space of infinitesimal deformations satisfying the
linearized equation. The second measures the failure of that linearized
equation to be surjective and is the obstruction space. Their alternating
dimension is zero, which agrees with the expected intersection dimension even
though the ordinary tangent space of the set-theoretic intersection is zero.

This computation must be presented with a caveat. The two-term complex is a
preview of the tangent complex of the derived intersection. At this point in
the seminar, that phrase is motivational terminology, not a formally
constructed object.

## The same example as a zero locus

The equations $s=t$ and $0=t^2$ identify the same fiber product with the zero
locus of
\[
  q\colon\mathbb R\longrightarrow\mathbb R,
  \qquad q(t)=t^2.
\]
This should be used as a change of viewpoint, not as a second independent
example. Compare it with $r(t)=t$:

1. Both zero loci have the same underlying set, namely one point.
2. For $r$, the complex $[\mathbb R\xrightarrow{1}\mathbb R]$ is acyclic. The
   zero is transverse.
3. For $q$, the complex $[\mathbb R\xrightarrow{0}\mathbb R]$ has a
   one-dimensional deformation space and a one-dimensional obstruction space.

This is the cleanest evidence for the claim that the underlying set forgets
essential information. The comparison also explains why one wants to retain
the equation, not merely its solutions.

The function $t^3$ is useful only as a written warning: it has the same
underlying zero set and the same linearized complex as $t^2$, although the
equation contains different higher-order information. Thus even the tangent
complex is not the whole derived structure. This point should be omitted from
the live route unless a question naturally raises it.

## A family in which kernels jump

Before mentioning elliptic operators, consider the family
\[
  L_t\colon\mathbb R\longrightarrow\mathbb R,
  \qquad L_t(u)=tu.
\]
For $t\neq0$, the kernel is zero; for $t=0$, it is all of $\mathbb R$. The
kernels therefore do not form a vector bundle over the parameter line. By
contrast, the family of complexes
\[
  [\mathbb R\xrightarrow{\,t\,}\mathbb R]
\]
is a perfectly smooth family of two-term complexes of trivial vector bundles.
Its total solution set is the singular zero locus
\[
  \{(t,u)\in\mathbb R^2\mid tu=0\},
\]
the union of the two coordinate axes.

This elementary family supplies the exact conceptual bridge to Pardon's
opening observation. For a family of elliptic operators, kernels and cokernels
can jump, while the family of Fredholm complexes remains the stable object.
No functional analysis is needed to understand the phenomenon.

## Bridge to nonlinear elliptic moduli problems

Steffens's Chapter 1 starts with a nonlinear elliptic differential operator
\[
  P\colon\Gamma(V)\longrightarrow\Gamma(F)
\]
over a compact manifold. The relevant finite-dimensional reduction statement
may be quoted in the following schematic form:

> Near a solution $s$ of $P(s)=0$, the solution set is locally given by the
> zero locus of a smooth map $f\colon\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R^k$, and the
> two-term complex $[D_sf]$ is quasi-isomorphic to the Fredholm deformation
> complex obtained by linearizing $P$ at $s$.

The speaker must call this a quoted analytic input. The exact hypotheses and
the meaning of the local comparison will be developed in the later analytic
unit. Talk 1 needs only the consequence: nonlinear elliptic moduli problems
locally produce the same sort of finite-dimensional zero loci already visible
in the examples.

There are then two problems which finite-dimensional reduction alone does not
solve:

1. Different choices produce different finite-dimensional reductions.
2. Reductions must be compared and glued on overlaps, including higher
   compatibility data.

These questions give the handoff to the semester. The first motivates an
intrinsic notion of derived smooth object; the second explains why coherent,
higher-categorical methods eventually enter.

## Recommended narrative and material budget

This is a deliberative budget, not yet the detailed talk outline.

1. **0--10 minutes:** Pose the geometric problem with transverse and tangent
   plane intersections.
2. **10--30 minutes:** State and prove the transverse fiber product theorem,
   including its tangent-space description.
3. **30--50 minutes:** Compute the tangent-parabola complex and rewrite it as
   the comparison between $t$ and $t^2$.
4. **49--59 minutes:** Study the family $L_t(u)=tu$, formulate the principle
   that complexes behave better in families than kernels, and only then
   synthesize the three requirements on a derived replacement.
5. **59--70 minutes:** Motivate nonlinear elliptic solution loci, linearize at
   a solution, and quote finite-dimensional reduction with its
   deformation--obstruction comparison.
6. **70--75 minutes:** Isolate non-uniqueness and coherent gluing as the two
   questions for the semester.
7. **75--90 minutes:** Reserve time for questions, recapitulation, and a short
   handoff. If earlier discussion has used this reserve, end directly with the
   four takeaways.

The first three segments form the mathematical core. The family and elliptic
bridge explain why that elementary core is not an artificial toy problem.

## Final takeaways

1. Transverse pullbacks are ordinary manifolds, and their tangent spaces are
   kernels of the linearized matching map.
2. At a non-transverse zero, the same linearized map has both a kernel and a
   cokernel. These record deformation and obstruction data invisible in the
   underlying zero set.
3. Two-term complexes behave well in families even when their kernels and
   cokernels do not form vector bundles.
4. Nonlinear elliptic moduli problems locally reduce to such zero-locus
   problems; the non-uniqueness and coherent gluing of these reductions are the
   global problems derived geometry must address.

The slogan “derived manifolds solve this” should not be counted as a fifth
takeaway. It is the question with which the talk ends.

## Primary references

1. Spivak, *Derived smooth manifolds*, Section 1, for broad motivation from
   non-transverse inverse images and derived intersection theory. The
   hypersurface inclusion-exclusion example is written reference only.
2. Steffens, *Derived differential geometry*, Chapter 1, especially Fact
   1.0.0.1 and Definitions 1.0.0.2--1.0.0.3, for finite-dimensional reduction,
   affine Kuranishi models, weak equivalences, and the two ensuing problems.
   Talk 1 uses the fact and the motivation, but not the definitions.
3. Pardon, *Representability in non-linear elliptic Fredholm analysis*,
   Sections 1--2, for the family-of-elliptic-complexes motivation and the
   representability viewpoint. The unfinished July 2026 manuscript is not used
   here.
4. A standard differential topology reference, for example Lee's
   *Introduction to smooth manifolds*, chapter on transversality and transverse
   intersections, for the transverse fiber product theorem. An exact edition
   and section should be fixed before writing the chapter.

## Deliberate exclusions

1. Do not define derived manifolds, derived $C^\infty$-rings, or Kuranishi
   models.
2. Do not construct tangent or cotangent complexes.
3. Do not discuss virtual fundamental classes or enumerative invariants.
4. Do not prove finite-dimensional reduction or introduce Sobolev spaces.
5. Do not develop a pseudo-holomorphic curve example. It may be named once as
   an eventual application.
6. Do not present Spivak's projective-hypersurface calculation in the live
   route.
