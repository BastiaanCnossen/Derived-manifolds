\section*{Goals}

The main goal of this seminar is to introduce the modern theory of derived manifolds, an elegant technical solution to various difficulties present in the classical theory of manifolds surrounding non-tranverse intersections. In the second half of the seminar we will present one application of derived manifolds: they are the correct home for moduli spaces of solutions to nonlinear elliptic partial differential equations. Such moduli spaces are of fundamental importance in much of modern geometric topology, such as symplectic topology, Floer theory, complex geometry and the theory of 4-manifolds. In each case, studying these moduli spaces leads to extremely interesting invariants. Specifically, our second goal will be to present an overview of a recent theorem of Pelle Steffens, which explains how to view such moduli spaces as derived manifolds: they end up being defined as solutions to natural moduli problems associated to the elliptic differential equation. We will not attempt to discuss complete proofs of every technical ingredient going into this theorem. Instead, we hope that by the end, participants will be ready to dig into the source material themselves.

The seminar is intended to be accessible to first-year PhD students joining the SFB. We have attempted to keep the seminar as self-contained and self-motivated as possible. In particular we do not suppose intimate knowledge of the theory of $\infty$-categories. Instead we hope to motivate it as a natural and indispensible language with which to discuss derived manifolds.  Nevertheless, the difficulty of talks does ramp up as we get to the later weeks of the program.

Each talk should last 90 minutes. The program below is a suggestion, but can be adapted as the semester progresses according to the needs and interests of the participants.

\section*{Program}

\Talk{1}{13 October}{Why derived manifolds?}
{Motivate the theory of derived manifolds}
{Prove that pullbacks of transverse intersection exist in the category of manifolds. Discuss the issues with understanding non-transverse intersection. As a running example, compare the zero-set of a line with the zero-set of a parabola. Using more examples, explain how remembering the differential as a two-term complex solves some, but not all, problems. This shows that the correct home for such intersections is necessarily ``derived''. With more examples, such as the zero-sets of $x^2$ vs $x^3$, explain how this is still not enough to fully capture the geometry of non-tranverse intersections. Give an informal discussion of the universal property of derived manifolds, as a maximal way to encode such non-transerve intersections.
	
End with giving an introduction to moduli spaces of elliptic equations. Discuss the local finite-dimensional reduction of an
elliptic equation, which allows one to present such moduli spaces locally as non-transverse intersections. Formulate the nonuniqueness and coherence problem for the resulting local presentations, again motivating derived manifolds.}
{\textcite[Section~1]{Spivak_Derived_Smooth_Manifolds};
\textcite[Chapter~1, especially Section~1.0.1]{Steffens_Derived_Differential_Geometry};
\textcite[Sections~1--2]{Pardon_Representability_Elliptic_Fredholm}.}

\Talk{2}{20 October}{Introduction to \Cinfty-rings}
{Familiarize ourselves with the algebraic structure used to generalize manifolds.}
{Define \Cinfty-rings and introduce their basic categorical properties. Prove that $\Cinfty(\mathbb R^n)$ is free on
$n$ generators. Prove Hadamard's lemma and discuss quotients of \Cinfty-rings. Discuss Borel's theorem. Define $\Cinfty(X)$ for $X\subset \mathbb R^n$ an arbitrary closed subset. Prove that the resulting functor from closed subsets of $\mathbb{R}^n$ to $\Cinfty$-rings is fully faithful.}
{\textcite[Chapter~I, Section~1]{Moerdijk_Reyes_Smooth_Infinitesimal_Analysis}.}

\Talk{3}{27 October}{\Cinfty-rings as generalized manifolds}
{Understand how \Cinfty-rings function as generalized manifolds.}
{Introduce coproducts of $\Cinfty$-rings. Prove Lemma 2.1 in Moerdijk--Reyes. Deduce that $\Cinfty(M)$ is finitely presented for every manifold $M$. Prove that $\Cinfty(M\times N) \simeq \Cinfty(M) \otimes \Cinfty(N)$. Prove that transverse pullbacks in manifolds induce pushouts of $\Cinfty$-rings.}
{\textcite[Chapter~I, Section~2]{Moerdijk_Reyes_Smooth_Infinitesimal_Analysis}.}

\Talk{4}{3 November}{From generalized manifolds to derived manifolds}
{Understand why the universal enlargement of manifolds by arbitrary pullbacks should be an $\infty$-category.}
{Formulate the 1-categorical universal property of finitely presented $\Cinfty$-rings. Use the results of the last talk to sketch a proof. Briefly introduce $\infty$-categories and homotopy pullbacks. Motivate the passage to $\infty$-categories for the theory of derived manifolds via abstract considerations and examples such as self-intersections. Discuss the process of ``animation''. Define derived manifolds via the $\infty$-categorical analogue of the previous universal property. State the theorem of Carchedi--Steffens identifying them with finitely presented derived $\Cinfty$-rings. Discuss as much of the proof as time permits.}
{\textcite{Carchedi_Steffens_Universal_Derived_Manifolds}.}

\Talk{5}{10 November}{Calculating derived intersections}
{Familiarize ourselves with derived manifolds by presenting many examples of non-transverse pullbacks}
{Define the derived zero locus as a pullback and calculate its animated \Cinfty-ring using a Koszul presentation. To do so introduce the model of animated rings given by \Cinfty-dga's, see 4.1.5.7 of Steffens' thesis. Give specific examples, such as in Spivak's Example 2.7. Alternatively, compare the zero loci of the equations $x$, $x^2$, and the zero map, carefully distinguishing the underlying set, classical truncation, and positive homotopy groups of the resulting derived manifolds. Extend these calculations to family-versions, given by zero loci of a vector-bundle section.}
{\textcite{Steffens_Derived_Differential_Geometry}, especially
Examples~4.1.5.10--4.1.5.11 and Construction~5.1.0.1;
\textcite[Sections~1--2]{Spivak_Derived_Smooth_Manifolds}.}

\Talk{6}{17 November}{Tangent and cotangent complexes}
{Define the tangent complex of a derived manifold and understand it's relevance to deformation and obstruction problems}
{First in the non-derived setting, discuss K\"ahler differentials on a $C^\infty$-ring and its connection to the (co)tangent bundle of a manifold. Motivate the passage to the cotangent complex. Define modules over an animated \Cinfty-ring through its underlying commutative ring spectrum. Introduce square-zero extensions and characterize the cotangent complex by derivations. Define the tangent complex by dualization. Use base change and transitivity to prove the tangent-complex of the derived zero locus of a section $s\colon U\to E$ locally has the form
\[
  [T_xU\longrightarrow E_x].
\]
Interpret its cohomology as deformation and obstruction spaces, compute the virtual dimension, and introduce affine quasi-smoothness.}
{\textcite[Section~5]{Steffens_Derived_Differential_Geometry};
\textcite{Pascarella_Cotangent_Complex_Derived_Differential_Geometry};
\textcite[Section~7]{Spivak_Derived_Smooth_Manifolds}.}

\Talk{7}{24 November}{Local derived geometry and Kuranishi charts}
{Define derived $\Cinfty$-schemes locally of finite presentation.  Connect this to the classical theory of Kuranishi charts, providing local models for the previous.}
{Introduce localizations of $\Cinfty$-rings and discuss the proof that they are flat. Discuss the spectrum of a $\Cinfty$-ring. Define derived \Cinfty-schemes (locally of finite presentation). Identify the subcategory of affine derived \Cinfty-schemes l.f.p.~with the opposite of derived manifolds. Define quasi-smooth derived \Cinfty-schemes. Introduce Kuranishi charts $(U,E,s)$ and their associated derived manifolds. Discuss the inverse function theorem for quasi-smooth derived \Cinfty-schemes. Interpret Kuranishi charts as local presentations of quasi-smooth derived objects. Emphasize the non-uniqueness of the chosen chart.}
{\textcite[Chapter~1 and Sections~4.1.3 and~5.1.1]{Steffens_Derived_Differential_Geometry}.}

\Talk{8}{1 December}{Derived bordism and fundamental classes}
{Define the theory of derived bordism and understand its benefits over classical bordism.}
{We follow Spivak. Explain how quasi-smooth derived manifolds are good for doing intersection theory. Define derived cobordism over $X$. State proposition~3.3, recalling it's proof for derived \Cinfty-rings. State proposition~3.4. Prove that the comparison map
	\[
	\Omega(X) \xrightarrow{\ \cong\ } \Omega^{\mathrm{der}}(X).
	\] 
	is an isomorphism by transverse pertubation. Deduce the intersection formula
	\[
	[A]\smile[B]=[A\mathbin{\times^{\mathrm{der}}_M}B]
	\]
	without transversality hypothesis. Discuss the derived self-intersection of the zero section of a vector bundle as the main example, recovering its Euler class.}
{\textcite[Sections~1--3]{Spivak_Derived_Smooth_Manifolds}.}

\Talk{9}{8 December}{Stacks and local representability}
{See how intrinsic moduli functors replace a manually chosen coherent
Kuranishi atlas.}
{Write down coordinate changes on double overlaps, homotopies on triple
overlaps, and the next compatibility on quadruple overlaps. Explain what the
\v{C}ech nerve of an already given derived object records, without asserting a
general strictification theorem for arbitrary Kuranishi data. Then introduce a
moduli functor of families, a representing object with its universal family,
and the locality of representability. Use the derived zero locus of a section
as the main example: maps into it are maps into the ambient manifold together
with a chosen equality between the section and zero.}
{\textcite[Section~1.0.2, Proposition~2.1.48, and
Corollary~2.1.49]{Steffens_Representability_Elliptic_Moduli};
\textcite[Introduction]{Pardon_Representability_Elliptic_Fredholm};
\textcite[Chapter~1]{Steffens_Derived_Differential_Geometry}, for the
coherence problem.}

\Talk{10}{15 December}{Section stacks and derived solution stacks}
{Construct the moduli stack of solutions to a differential operator}
{Recall briefly the motivation from moduli spaces of elliptic PDE's. Define the Weyl restriction on stacks. For a family $W\to M\to S$ of derived stacks, define the stack of sections and the relative jet stack. Define a finite-order differential operator as a map of jet stacks. Define jet prolongation and the induced map on stacks of sections. Define differential moduli problems and their solution stacks. Finally define an \emph{elliptic} differential moduli problem. State the identification of the tangent complex of a section stack in Proposition~3.2.22. Deduce a presentation of the tangent complex of the solution stack in terms of the linearized differential operator. Use
\[
  P(u)=\Delta u+u^2
\]

on a closed connected Riemannian manifold as a running example.}
{\textcite[Definition~3.2.1, Proposition~3.2.22, Definition~3.3.7,
	Proposition~3.3.9, and
	Definition~3.4.2]{Steffens_Representability_Elliptic_Moduli};
	\textcite[Section~4]{Pardon_Representability_Elliptic_Fredholm}.}

\Talk{11}{12 January}{Fredholm equations and finite-dimensional reduction}
{Turn one infinite-dimensional elliptic equation into a finite-dimensional derived zero-locus model.}
{Explain why Sobolev completion turns the elliptic linearization into a
Fredholm operator. Choose a finite-dimensional obstruction space mapping
isomorphically to its cokernel, apply the Banach implicit function theorem to
the augmented problem, and use elliptic regularity to recover smooth
solutions. Carry out the reduction for $\Delta u+u^2=0$ near $u=0$: split into
constant and mean-zero functions, obtain the obstruction map $a\mapsto a^2$
exactly, and compare its tangent complex with
$[\mathbb R\xrightarrow{0}\mathbb R]$. End by identifying the gap between a
chosen Kuranishi model and representability of the intrinsic solution stack.}
{\textcite[Section~1.0.1, Lemmas~3.4.5--3.4.6, and the analytic part of the
proof of Theorem~3.4.3]{Steffens_Representability_Elliptic_Moduli};
\textcite[Section~2 and the first step of the proof sketch of
Theorem~5.1]{Pardon_Representability_Elliptic_Fredholm}.}

\Talk{12}{19 January}{Elliptic representability I: the smooth-to-derived bridge}
{Explain why Sobolev analysis proves an intrinsic submersiveness statement in
derived smooth geometry.}
{State Steffens's relative elliptic representability theorem as the
destination. Localize on the smooth-stack base, pass to vector-bundle charts
for the relevant section stacks, and reduce to one nonlinear PDE over a fixed
compact manifold. Construct a functorial Sobolev tower and compatible
obstruction augmentation. Test the augmented smooth operator after pullback
from an ordinary manifold, then use nonlinear and linear elliptic regularity
to prove that the resulting tower is eventually locally constant. Conclude
that the augmented operator is submersive after passage to derived geometry.
Stop before forming its finite-dimensional derived zero locus.}
{\textcite{Steffens_Representability_Elliptic_Moduli}, especially
Proposition~2.2.18, Proposition~3.2.22, Lemma~3.4.4, Theorem~3.4.3, and
Lemmas~3.4.5--3.4.6.}

\Talk{13}{26 January}{Elliptic representability II: derived charts and globalization}
{Turn the derived-submersive augmented equation into local
finite-dimensional charts and complete the representability theorem.}
{Form the ordinary finite-dimensional zero fiber of the augmented operator,
project it to the obstruction space, and recover the original problem as a
finite-dimensional derived zero locus. Identify this object with an open
restriction of the intrinsic solution stack by pasting pullback squares,
compare its tangent complex with the elliptic deformation-obstruction complex,
and prove quasi-smoothness. Vary the solution and apply the effective
open-atlas criterion to complete Steffens's theorem. End with a concise
comparison with Pardon's alternate route through ordinary regularity, proper
section stacks on derived tests, and finite-dimensional parameter thickening.
Treat the published argument as a proof sketch rather than as a second full
proof.}
{\textcite[Proposition~2.1.48, Corollary~2.1.49, Theorem~3.4.3, and
Remark~3.4.7]{Steffens_Representability_Elliptic_Moduli};
\textcite[Sections~4--5, especially Theorem~5.1, Proposition~5.2, and
Lemma~5.3]{Pardon_Representability_Elliptic_Fredholm}.}

\Talk{14}{2 February}{Pseudo-holomorphic curves as a derived moduli problem}
{See the complete representability architecture operate on its principal
geometric application.}
{Describe families of maps from smooth compact Riemann surfaces to an almost
complex manifold and formulate the Cauchy--Riemann equation as a finite-order
differential moduli problem. Identify its linearization as a Cauchy--Riemann
type operator and prove ellipticity by calculating the principal symbol. Write
the relative deformation-obstruction complex and interpret its cohomology.
Retain families and automorphisms through the smooth-stack base, then apply
Talks~12--13 to obtain relative representability. End the seminar by separating
this smooth-domain result from compactification by nodal curves, orientations,
and the construction of virtual classes, recalling the precise contribution
of the bordism interlude.}
{\textcite[Sections~2.1, 2.3--2.4, and
3.3--3.4]{Wendl_Lectures_Holomorphic_Curves};
\textcite[Section~4]{Steffens_Representability_Elliptic_Moduli};
\textcite[Section~4]{Pardon_Representability_Elliptic_Fredholm}.}
