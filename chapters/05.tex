\chapter{Calculating derived intersections}
\label[chapter]{chap:Derived_Zero_Loci_And_Tangent_Complexes}

The preceding chapter constructed the $\infty$-category of derived manifolds
and identified it algebraically:
\[
  \mathrm{DMfd}
  \simeq
  \operatorname{Alg}_{\Cinfty}(\An)_{\mathrm{fp}}\catop.
\]
We therefore know that arbitrary finite pullbacks of manifolds exist in
$\mathrm{DMfd}$. If the maps are transverse, the result agrees with the
ordinary manifold pullback. If they are not transverse, the universal
property still supplies an object, but it does not yet tell us what that
object contains.

This chapter makes the new pullbacks calculable. Its protagonist is the
derived zero locus of a smooth map. We first express it as a pushout of
animated $\Cinfty$-rings, then present that pushout by a Koszul algebra, and
finally compare three equations:
\[
  x,
  \qquad
  x^2,
  \qquad
  0.
\]
The examples are deliberately elementary. Their purpose is to separate three
levels of information which become easy to conflate once the notation grows
more sophisticated: the underlying zero set, the classical truncation, and
the full animated function algebra.

We will not yet extract either a bordism class or a stable linearization from
the derived zero locus. The next chapter first asks how a compact derived zero
locus compares with a transverse perturbation. The following chapter then
studies its intrinsic first-order approximation. These divisions are
important: the Koszul algebra calculated here contains nonlinear information
which both later constructions deliberately simplify.

\section{From formal pullbacks to derived zero loci}
\label[section]{sec:Calculating_Derived_Zero_Loci}

\subsection{The self-intersection test}
\label[subsection]{sec:Derived_Self_Intersection_Test}

Return to the self-intersection of the $x$-axis in $\R^2$. Write
\[
  i\colon \R\longrightarrow\R^2,
  \qquad
  i(x)=(x,0).
\]
The ordinary set-theoretic fiber product of $i$ with itself is again the
$x$-axis. The same collapse occurs in ordinary smooth algebra. If
\[
  B=\Cinfty(\R^2),
  \qquad
  A=B/(y)\cong\Cinfty(\R),
\]
then
\[
  A\amalg_B A
  \cong
  A.
\]
The two copies of the equation $y=0$ have become indistinguishable. This is
exactly the defect isolated in the previous chapter: an ordinary pushout of
sets of algebraic data remembers the imposed relation, but not the ways in
which two copies of that relation agree.

The derived self-intersection exists by the universal property of
$\mathrm{DMfd}$. What remains is to calculate its animated function algebra.
We begin in a slightly more general form which will be reused throughout the
seminar.

\subsection{The intrinsic definition}
\label[subsection]{sec:Derived_Zero_Locus_Pullback_Pushout}

Let $U$ be a manifold and let
\[
  f=(f_1,\ldots,f_k)\colon U\longrightarrow\R^k
\]
be smooth. Its ordinary zero set is the set-theoretic pullback of $f$ along
the origin. We form the corresponding pullback in derived manifolds.

\begin{definition}[Derived zero locus]
  \label[definition]{def:Derived_Zero_Locus_Function}
  The \emph{derived zero locus} of $f$ is the pullback
  \[
  \begin{tikzcd}
    Z^{\mathrm{der}}(f) \rar \dar \drar[pullback]
      & * \dar["0"] \\
    U \rar["f"'] & \R^k
  \end{tikzcd}
  \]
  in $\mathrm{DMfd}$.
\end{definition}

All pullbacks in this chapter are formed in an $\infty$-category. We do not
add a separate adjective such as ``homotopy'' to the construction.

Passing contravariantly to animated function algebras turns the pullback into
a pushout:
\[
\begin{tikzcd}
  \Cinfty(\R^k) \rar["f^*"] \dar["0^*"'] \drar[pushout]
    & \Cinfty(U) \dar \\
  \R \rar
    & \mathcal O\bigl(Z^{\mathrm{der}}(f)\bigr).
\end{tikzcd}
\]
We write the lower-right corner as
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:Derived_Zero_Locus_Animated_Pushout}
  \mathcal O\bigl(Z^{\mathrm{der}}(f)\bigr)
  \simeq
  \Cinfty(U)
  \mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}^{\mathbb L}_{\Cinfty(\R^k)}}
  \R.
\end{equation}
The notation $\otimes^{\mathbb L}$ means the displayed pushout in animated
$\Cinfty$-rings. It is not a second definition of the derived zero locus.
The geometric pullback is intrinsic, and
\Cref{eq:Derived_Zero_Locus_Animated_Pushout} is its algebraic expression.

This observation also explains why the passage to animated rings is doing
real work. In ordinary $\Cinfty$-rings the same diagram has a pushout, but
that pushout retains only an ordinary quotient. The animated pushout retains
the entire universal gluing datum.

\subsection{Three levels of information}
\label[subsection]{sec:Three_Levels_Derived_Zero_Locus}

There are three related objects which we will keep separate throughout the
chapter.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item The \emph{underlying zero set} $f^{-1}(0)$ records which points solve
    the equation.
  \item The \emph{classical truncation} is obtained by applying $\pi_0$ to
    the animated function algebra. It records the ordinary smooth algebra of
    the equation, including nilpotents.
  \item The \emph{full animated function algebra} also includes the positive
    homotopy groups $\pi_i$ for $i>0$.
\end{enumerate}

Each passage forgets information:
\[
  \text{animated function algebra}
  \longmapsto
  \pi_0
  \longmapsto
  \text{set of real points}.
\]
The two forgetting steps diagnose different phenomena. The equations $x=0$
and $x^2=0$ have the same underlying zero set, but their classical
truncations differ. On the other hand, the derived zero locus of the zero
function has the same classical truncation as its underlying manifold, but
its animated function algebra has positive homotopy.

This is the organizing principle for the calculations below. Derived
structure is not synonymous with either nilpotence in $\pi_0$ or positive
homotopy. Both can occur, and they measure different failures of ordinary
smoothness.

\section{The Koszul presentation}
\label[section]{sec:Koszul_Presentation_Derived_Zero_Locus}

\subsection{A computational bridge}
\label[subsection]{sec:Smooth_Dold_Kan_Bridge}

The intrinsic pushout in
\Cref{eq:Derived_Zero_Locus_Animated_Pushout} is not yet explicit. To
calculate it, we use a comparison between animated $\Cinfty$-rings and chain
$\Cinfty$-algebras.

A connective chain $\Cinfty$-algebra is a nonnegatively graded
graded-commutative differential algebra
\[
  A_0\xleftarrow{\partial}A_1
  \xleftarrow{\partial}A_2
  \xleftarrow{\partial}\cdots
\]
whose degree-zero algebra carries compatible smooth functional calculus. A
quasi-isomorphism is a morphism inducing isomorphisms on homology.

\begin{fact}[Smooth Dold--Kan comparison]
  \label[fact]{fact:Smooth_Dold_Kan_Comparison}
  The $\infty$-category obtained from connective chain
  $\Cinfty$-algebras by inverting quasi-isomorphisms is equivalent to the
  $\infty$-category of animated $\Cinfty$-rings. Under this equivalence, the
  homology groups of a chain $\Cinfty$-algebra are the homotopy groups of the
  corresponding animated $\Cinfty$-ring.
\end{fact}

This is the smooth Dold--Kan correspondence of Steffens,
Theorem~4.1.5.7, translated into our homological grading convention
\cite[Theorem~4.1.5.7]{Steffens_Derived_Differential_Geometry}. We use it only
as a computational bridge. The intrinsic definition remains the pullback in
$\mathrm{DMfd}$, or equivalently the animated pushout of
\Cref{eq:Derived_Zero_Locus_Animated_Pushout}. A chain algebra is one
convenient presentation of that object.

\subsection{The algebra attached to an equation}
\label[subsection]{sec:Koszul_Algebra_Attached_To_Equation}

For $f=(f_1,\ldots,f_k)$, introduce generators
$e_1,\ldots,e_k$ in homological degree one and define
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:Koszul_Algebra_Function}
  K(f)
  =
  \bigl(
    \Cinfty(U)\otimes\Lambda(e_1,\ldots,e_k),
    \partial e_i=f_i
  \bigr).
\end{equation}
Thus
\[
  e_ie_j=-e_je_i,
  \qquad
  e_i^2=0,
\]
and the graded Leibniz rule determines the differential on products. For
example,
\[
  \partial(ge_i)=gf_i
\]
for $g\in\Cinfty(U)$. With two generators one has
\[
  \partial(ge_1e_2)=g(f_1e_2-f_2e_1).
\]

The formula has a simple interpretation. The generator $e_i$ is a degree-one
witness for the equation $f_i=0$, and the differential sends that witness to
the function which is required to vanish. This interpretation is useful for
reading the algebra, but it should not be mistaken for extra chosen structure
on the derived zero locus. The Koszul algebra is a presentation of an
intrinsic animated ring.

\begin{theorem}[Koszul presentation of a derived zero locus]
  \label[theorem]{thm:Koszul_Presentation_Derived_Zero_Locus}
  Let $f=(f_1,\ldots,f_k)\colon U\to\R^k$ be smooth. The animated
  $\Cinfty$-ring of its derived zero locus is represented by $K(f)$. In
  particular,
  \[
    \pi_i\mathcal O\bigl(Z^{\mathrm{der}}(f)\bigr)
    \cong
    H_iK(f)
  \]
  for every $i\geq0$.
\end{theorem}

The proof has two steps. First resolve the evaluation map
\[
  \Cinfty(\R^k)\longrightarrow\R
\]
by adjoining degree-one generators whose differentials are the coordinate
functions. Then extend scalars along
\[
  f^*\colon\Cinfty(\R^k)\longrightarrow\Cinfty(U).
\]
The coordinate functions become $f_1,\ldots,f_k$, which gives
\Cref{eq:Koszul_Algebra_Function}. The full argument is recorded in
\Cref{sec:Supplement_Koszul_Calculation}. Steffens gives the calculation in
Examples~4.1.5.10--4.1.5.11
\cite[Examples~4.1.5.10--4.1.5.11]{Steffens_Derived_Differential_Geometry}.

\subsection{The classical truncation}
\label[subsection]{sec:Classical_Truncation_Koszul_Algebra}

The degree-zero homology can be read off immediately. Since the only
boundaries in degree zero are sums of the form
\[
  \sum_{i=1}^k g_if_i,
  \qquad
  g_i\in\Cinfty(U),
\]
we obtain
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:H_Zero_Koszul_Quotient}
  H_0K(f)
  \cong
  \frac{\Cinfty(U)}{(f_1,\ldots,f_k)}.
\end{equation}
Thus the classical truncation is the ordinary algebraic zero locus studied in
Chapters~2--4.

The higher homology asks a different question. A degree-one cycle is a
relation
\[
  \sum_i g_if_i=0
\]
among the equations. It contributes to $H_1$ only if it is not generated by
the evident antisymmetric relations coming from degree two. Higher degrees
record higher relations among relations. This is the first reason the Koszul
presentation is useful: it turns the equality data retained by an animated
pushout into explicit algebra.

When the equations form a regular sequence, the positive Koszul homology
vanishes. Transverse equations are the smooth geometric instance of this
behavior. The converse intuition must be treated carefully, however. The
vanishing of positive homology does not imply that the classical truncation
is a manifold, as the equation $x^2=0$ will show.

\section{Three diagnostic calculations}
\label[section]{sec:Three_Koszul_Calculations}

We now compare a transverse equation, a singular equation with discrete
animated algebra, and a genuinely derived self-intersection. No new machinery
enters. The point is to learn how to read the three levels introduced in
\Cref{sec:Three_Levels_Derived_Zero_Locus}.

\subsection{A transverse zero}
\label[subsection]{sec:Koszul_Transverse_Zero}

\begin{example}[The equation $x=0$]
  \label[example]{ex:Koszul_Transverse_Zero}
  Let $f\colon\R\to\R$ be the identity. Its Koszul algebra has underlying
  two-term chain complex
  \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:Koszul_Complex_X}
    \Cinfty(\R)e
    \xrightarrow{x}
    \Cinfty(\R),
  \end{equation}
  where the right-hand term is in degree zero.
\end{example}

Multiplication by $x$ is injective. Indeed, if $xg(x)=0$, then $g$ vanishes
away from the origin and hence, by continuity, also at the origin. Evaluation
at zero induces an isomorphism
\[
  \frac{\Cinfty(\R)}{(x)}
  \iso
  \R.
\]
Consequently,
\[
  H_0K(x)\cong\R,
  \qquad
  H_iK(x)=0\quad(i>0).
\]

The derived zero locus is therefore the ordinary point. At all three levels
there is nothing new:
\[
  \text{underlying set }\{0\},
  \qquad
  \pi_0\cong\R,
  \qquad
  \pi_{>0}=0.
\]
This is the algebraic form of transverse agreement. Derived geometry does not
alter a pullback which was already transverse.

\subsection{A singular but discrete zero locus}
\label[subsection]{sec:Koszul_Square_Zero}

Now replace the identity by its square.

\begin{example}[The equation $x^2=0$]
  \label[example]{ex:Koszul_Square_Zero}
  Let $f(x)=x^2$. Its Koszul algebra has underlying two-term chain complex
  \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:Koszul_Complex_X_Squared}
    \Cinfty(\R)e
    \xrightarrow{x^2}
    \Cinfty(\R).
  \end{equation}
\end{example}

Multiplication by $x^2$ is again injective. If $x^2g(x)=0$, then $g$ vanishes
for $x\neq0$ and hence also at zero. It follows that
\[
  H_0K(x^2)
  \cong
  \frac{\Cinfty(\R)}{(x^2)},
  \qquad
  H_iK(x^2)=0\quad(i>0).
\]
Thus the animated function algebra is discrete.

It is nevertheless not the algebra of an ordinary manifold. Let $\bar x$ be
the class of $x$ in the quotient. Then $\bar x^2=0$, but $\bar x\neq0$. If
$x$ lay in the ideal $(x^2)$, there would be a smooth function $h$ satisfying
$x=x^2h$. Away from zero this would force $h(x)=1/x$, which does not extend
continuously across the origin. Hence the quotient contains a nonzero
nilpotent element.

The equations $x$ and $x^2$ therefore have the same underlying zero set
\[
  \{x=0\}=\{x^2=0\}=\{0\},
\]
but their classical truncations differ:
\[
  \frac{\Cinfty(\R)}{(x)}\cong\R,
  \qquad
  \frac{\Cinfty(\R)}{(x^2)}\not\cong\R.
\]
The set of solutions has forgotten the infinitesimal thickening retained by
the ordinary quotient.

This example establishes an important boundary:

\begin{quote}
  Nontransversality does not by itself force positive homotopy groups in the
  animated function algebra.
\end{quote}

The singularity of $x^2=0$ is already present in $\pi_0$. Positive homotopy is
not the definition of singularity in derived geometry.

\subsection{The zero equation and self-intersection}
\label[subsection]{sec:Koszul_Zero_Section}

The third calculation exhibits information which $\pi_0$ itself forgets.

\begin{example}[The zero function]
  \label[example]{ex:Koszul_Zero_Section}
  Let $U$ be a manifold and consider
  \[
    0\colon U\longrightarrow\R.
  \]
  Its Koszul algebra is
  \[
    K(0)
    =
    \bigl(\Cinfty(U)\otimes\Lambda(e),\partial e=0\bigr).
  \]
  Since the differential vanishes,
  \[
    H_0K(0)\cong\Cinfty(U),
    \qquad
    H_1K(0)\cong\Cinfty(U).
  \]
\end{example}

The ordinary zero locus of the zero function is all of $U$. The classical
truncation of its derived zero locus is also $U$. Nevertheless,
\[
  \pi_1\mathcal O\bigl(Z^{\mathrm{der}}(0)\bigr)
  \cong
  \Cinfty(U)
\]
is nonzero. Thus the passage to $\pi_0$ forgets a genuine derived class.

Geometrically, the same object is the derived self-intersection of the zero
section of the trivial line bundle $U\times\R\to U$. For $U=\R$, this is the
self-intersection of the $x$-axis with which the chapter began. The
degree-one generator records the repeated normal equation which disappeared
from the ordinary pushout.

This language requires one qualification. The generator $e$ belongs to the
chosen Koszul presentation, not canonically to the derived manifold as a
named element. What is intrinsic is the resulting animated function algebra,
including its nonzero $\pi_1$. Another presentation can encode the same
derived object differently.

\subsection{What the examples distinguish}
\label[subsection]{sec:What_Three_Koszul_Examples_Distinguish}

The three calculations, together with their ordinary benchmarks, give the
following comparison:
\[
\begin{array}{c|c|c|c}
  \text{object}
    & \text{underlying set}
    & \pi_0\mathcal O
    & \pi_{>0}\mathcal O
  \\
  \hline
  Z^{\mathrm{der}}(x)
    & \{0\}
    & \R
    & 0
  \\
  Z^{\mathrm{der}}(x^2)
    & \{0\}
    & \Cinfty(\R)/(x^2)
    & 0
  \\
  U
    & U
    & \Cinfty(U)
    & 0
  \\
  Z^{\mathrm{der}}(0\colon U\to\R)
    & U
    & \Cinfty(U)
    & \pi_1\cong\Cinfty(U).
\end{array}
\]

The first two rows show that the underlying set does not determine the
classical truncation. The final two rows show that the classical truncation
does not determine the derived object. These are two independent losses of
information.

One should therefore resist arranging the examples on a single scale from
``less derived'' to ``more derived.'' The equation $x^2=0$ is singular through
a nilpotent in degree zero. The zero equation has an ordinary truncation but a
nontrivial class in degree one. The Koszul presentation detects both
phenomena, but distinguishes them.

\section{Vector-bundle zero loci and the next question}
\label[section]{sec:Vector_Bundle_Zero_Loci_And_Handoff}

\subsection{From functions to sections}
\label[subsection]{sec:From_Functions_To_Vector_Bundle_Sections}

Most geometric moduli problems are not presented by a single map to
$\R^k$. They are presented as the zero locus of a section
\[
  s\colon U\longrightarrow E
\]
of a finite-rank vector bundle $q\colon E\to U$. The correct pullback is
\[
\begin{tikzcd}
  Z^{\mathrm{der}}(s) \rar \dar \drar[pullback]
    & U \dar["0"] \\
  U \rar["s"'] & E,
\end{tikzcd}
\]
where the right-hand map is the zero section. Consequently,
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:Vector_Bundle_Derived_Zero_Locus_Pushout}
  \mathcal O\bigl(Z^{\mathrm{der}}(s)\bigr)
  \simeq
  \Cinfty(U)
  \mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}^{\mathbb L}_{\Cinfty(E)}}
  \Cinfty(U),
\end{equation}
where the two structure maps are $s^*$ and $0^*$.

After choosing a local frame for $E$, the section is given by a tuple of
smooth functions and the previous Koszul presentation applies. There is also
a coordinate-free global form.

\begin{proposition}[Vector-bundle Koszul presentation]
  \label[proposition]{prop:Vector_Bundle_Koszul_Presentation}
  The animated function algebra of $Z^{\mathrm{der}}(s)$ is represented by
  \[
    \bigl(
      \Gamma(U,\Lambda^\bullet E^\vee),
      \partial_s
    \bigr),
  \]
  where $\partial_s$ is contraction with $s$. In degree one,
  \[
    \partial_s(t)=t(s)
  \]
  for $t\in\Gamma(U,E^\vee)$.
\end{proposition}

In a local frame $e_1,\ldots,e_k$ of $E^\vee$, write
\[
  s=(s_1,\ldots,s_k).
\]
Then contraction with $s$ sends $e_i$ to $s_i$, so the proposition reduces
locally to
\[
  \bigl(
    \Cinfty(U)\otimes\Lambda(e_1,\ldots,e_k),
    \partial e_i=s_i
  \bigr).
\]
The coordinate-free notation makes clear that a change of frame changes the
presentation but not the derived zero locus. A proof of the global statement
is given in
\Cref{sec:Supplement_Vector_Bundle_Koszul_Formula}.

\subsection{The derived object versus a transverse perturbation}
\label[subsection]{sec:Derived_Object_Versus_Transverse_Perturbation}

The Koszul algebra contains the full section $s$, not only its derivative at
a zero. For example, the functions $x^2$ and $x^3$ have the same derivative
at the origin, but their classical zero-locus algebras
\[
  \Cinfty(\R)/(x^2)
  \qquad\text{and}\qquad
  \Cinfty(\R)/(x^3)
\]
are different. No construction using only the first derivative can recover
this distinction.

Suppose now that $U$ is compact. A small perturbation of $s$ may be chosen
transverse to the zero section, and its zero locus is then an ordinary smooth
manifold. Perturbation has simplified the geometry, but it has also discarded
some of the information visible in the Koszul algebra. In particular, there
is no reason for the unperturbed derived zero locus to be equivalent to the
ordinary zero locus of the perturbation.

This leads to the next problem:

\begin{question}
  \label[question]{question:Derived_Zero_Locus_Transverse_Perturbation}
  Does a compact derived zero locus determine the same bordism class as the
  ordinary manifold obtained from a transverse perturbation?
\end{question}

The next chapter answers this question by regarding a homotopy from $s$ to a
transverse perturbation as a derived cobordism. This will extract a classical
intersection-theoretic class while retaining the unperturbed derived zero
locus as the canonical geometric representative. Stable linearization comes
one chapter later and recovers finer deformation and obstruction information
which bordism forgets.

\section{Summary and supplementary materials}
\label[section]{sec:Derived_Zero_Loci_Summary_And_Supplement}

\subsection{Takeaways}
\label[subsection]{sec:Derived_Zero_Loci_Takeaways}

The live route through the chapter can be compressed into six statements.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item A derived zero locus is a pullback in $\mathrm{DMfd}$ and hence a
    pushout of animated $\Cinfty$-rings after passing to functions.
  \item The Koszul algebra
    \[
      \bigl(
        \Cinfty(U)\otimes\Lambda(e_1,\ldots,e_k),
        \partial e_i=f_i
      \bigr)
    \]
    is a concrete chain presentation of that animated pushout.
  \item The underlying zero set, the classical truncation, and the full
    animated algebra are three different levels of information.
  \item The equations $x$ and $x^2$ have the same zero set but different
    classical truncations. In particular, singularity does not require
    positive homotopy.
  \item The derived zero locus of the zero function has the same classical
    truncation as $U$ but a nonzero degree-one homotopy group.
  \item A vector-bundle section has a coordinate-free Koszul presentation by
    exterior powers of its dual bundle. For compact zero loci, the next
    chapter compares this derived object with a transverse perturbation in
    bordism.
\end{enumerate}

The remaining subsections record proofs, grading conventions, and references.
They are not part of the default live route.

\subsection{Supplement: why the Koszul presentation computes the pushout}
\label[subsection]{sec:Supplement_Koszul_Calculation}

We prove the calculation quoted in
\Cref{thm:Koszul_Presentation_Derived_Zero_Locus}. Let
$x_1,\ldots,x_k$ be the coordinate functions on $\R^k$ and define
\[
  Q_k
  =
  \bigl(
    \Cinfty(\R^k)\otimes\Lambda(e_1,\ldots,e_k),
    \partial e_i=x_i
  \bigr).
\]
Evaluation at the origin extends to an augmentation
\[
  Q_k\longrightarrow\R
\]
which sends every $e_i$ to zero.

\begin{lemma}
  \label[lemma]{lem:Coordinate_Koszul_Resolution_Origin}
  The augmentation $Q_k\to\R$ is a quasi-isomorphism.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
  We verify the regularity needed for the Koszul calculation. Hadamard's lemma
  says that every smooth function $g$ which vanishes on the hyperplane
  $x_1=0$ can be written as $g=x_1h$ for a smooth function $h$. Hence
  restriction to that hyperplane induces an isomorphism
  \[
    \frac{\Cinfty(\R^k)}{(x_1)}
    \iso
    \Cinfty(\R^{k-1}).
  \]
  Multiplication by $x_1$ is injective. Repeating the argument after
  restriction shows that multiplication by $x_i$ is injective modulo
  $(x_1,\ldots,x_{i-1})$ and that
  \[
    \frac{\Cinfty(\R^k)}{(x_1,\ldots,x_i)}
    \iso
    \Cinfty(\R^{k-i}).
  \]
  Thus $x_1,\ldots,x_k$ is a regular sequence in the order displayed. The
  usual inductive proof of exactness of its Koszul complex applies. The
  degree-zero homology is
  \[
    \frac{\Cinfty(\R^k)}{(x_1,\ldots,x_k)}
    \cong
    \R,
  \]
  and the positive homology vanishes.
\end{proof}

The lemma makes $Q_k$ a chain-algebra resolution of the evaluation algebra
$\R$. Extend scalars along
\[
  f^*\colon\Cinfty(\R^k)\longrightarrow\Cinfty(U).
\]
The result is
\begin{align*}
  \Cinfty(U)\otimes_{\Cinfty(\R^k)}Q_k
  &\cong
  \Cinfty(U)\otimes\Lambda(e_1,\ldots,e_k),\\
  \partial e_i&=f_i.
\end{align*}
This is $K(f)$. By the smooth Dold--Kan comparison, it represents the
animated pushout in
\Cref{eq:Derived_Zero_Locus_Animated_Pushout}.

The resolution is only a calculation device. The resulting animated
$\Cinfty$-ring is characterized intrinsically by the pushout, independently
of the chosen presentation.

\subsection{Supplement: the coordinate-free vector-bundle formula}
\label[subsection]{sec:Supplement_Vector_Bundle_Koszul_Formula}

We prove \Cref{prop:Vector_Bundle_Koszul_Presentation}. Let
$q\colon E\to U$ be the bundle projection and let
\[
  \tau\in\Gamma(E,q^*E)
\]
be the tautological section, given by
\[
  \tau(e)=e\in E_{q(e)}.
\]
Consider the chain $\Cinfty(E)$-algebra
\[
  Q_E
  =
  \Cinfty(E)
  \otimes_{\Cinfty(U)}
  \Gamma(U,\Lambda^\bullet E^\vee),
\]
with differential given by contraction with $\tau$. For
$t\in\Gamma(U,E^\vee)$, the function $\partial t\in\Cinfty(E)$ is
\[
  (\partial t)(e)=t_{q(e)}(e).
\]
Evaluation along the zero section gives an augmentation
\[
  Q_E\longrightarrow\Cinfty(U).
\]

The factorization
\[
  \Cinfty(E)
  \longrightarrow
  Q_E
  \longrightarrow
  \Cinfty(U)
\]
is a resolution suitable for calculating the animated pushout along the zero
section. For a trivial rank-$k$ bundle, it is the coordinate Koszul
resolution with differential $\partial e_i=y_i$ in the fiber coordinates.
For a general vector bundle, the assertion follows by functoriality and the
fact that a vector bundle is a retract of a trivial bundle. This is the global
resolution constructed in Steffens, Example~4.1.5.11
\cite[Example~4.1.5.11]{Steffens_Derived_Differential_Geometry}.

Extend scalars along
\[
  s^*\colon\Cinfty(E)\longrightarrow\Cinfty(U).
\]
The resulting chain algebra is
\begin{align*}
  \Cinfty(U)\otimes_{\Cinfty(E)}Q_E
  &\cong
  \Gamma(U,\Lambda^\bullet E^\vee),\\
  \partial_s(t)&=t(s).
\end{align*}
By the smooth Dold--Kan comparison, it represents the animated pushout in
\Cref{eq:Vector_Bundle_Derived_Zero_Locus_Pushout}.

\subsection{Supplement: grading conventions}
\label[subsection]{sec:Derived_Zero_Loci_Grading_Conventions}

The Koszul presentation is a nonnegatively graded chain algebra whose
differential lowers degree. Its homology $H_i$ presents the homotopy group
$\pi_i$ of the animated function algebra. Thus the exterior generator in the
zero-function example occupies homological degree one and represents a class
in $\pi_1$.

The next chapter will write cotangent and tangent complexes
cohomologically. A quasi-smooth cotangent complex occupies degrees $-1$ and
$0$, while its dual tangent complex occupies degrees $0$ and $1$. The
degree-one Koszul class of the zero function and the degree-one obstruction
space of its tangent complex therefore belong to different objects and have
different meanings.

Steffens uses homological conventions for chain presentations. Spivak also
uses conventions which require translation when passing between function
algebras and deformation complexes. Every displayed tangent complex in this
manuscript follows the cohomological convention stated above.

\subsection{Related literature}
\label[subsection]{sec:Derived_Zero_Loci_Related_Literature}

The smooth Dold--Kan comparison which permits connective chain
$\Cinfty$-algebras to be used as presentations of animated
$\Cinfty$-rings is Steffens, Theorem~4.1.5.7
\cite[Theorem~4.1.5.7]{Steffens_Derived_Differential_Geometry}. His
Examples~4.1.5.10--4.1.5.11 give the explicit Koszul presentations for a
smooth function and a vector-bundle section
\cite[Examples~4.1.5.10--4.1.5.11]{Steffens_Derived_Differential_Geometry}.

The universal algebraic presentation of derived manifolds used at the start
of the chapter is Carchedi--Steffens, Corollary~5.4
\cite[Corollary~5.4]{Carchedi_Steffens_Universal_Derived_Manifolds}. Their
universal-property theorem explains why the animated pushouts calculated here
are intrinsic derived manifolds rather than a separate class of differential
graded objects.

Spivak develops derived zero loci and their geometric intersection theory in
an earlier axiomatic construction of derived smooth manifolds
\cite[Sections~1--3]{Spivak_Derived_Smooth_Manifolds}. The present chapter
uses the Carchedi--Steffens universal presentation for the intrinsic object
and Steffens's smooth Dold--Kan comparison for the calculation.
