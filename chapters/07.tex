\chapter{Stable linearization and tangent complexes}
\label[chapter]{chap:Stable_Linearization_And_Tangent_Complexes}

Talk 5 studied a derived zero locus as a nonlinear animated algebra. For a
smooth function $f\colon U\to\R$, the Koszul presentation remembers the
function itself:
\[
  K(f)=\bigl(\Cinfty(U)\otimes\Lambda(e),\partial e=f\bigr).
\]
Talk 6 then showed that derived bordism deliberately forgets much of this
nonlinear information. A derived zero locus may be replaced, within its
bordism class, by a transverse perturbation.

This chapter asks for something between those two extremes. We want an
intrinsic first-order approximation which remembers both infinitesimal
solutions and the failure of transversality. The ordinary tangent space gives
only
\[
  \ker(D_xf).
\]
At a nontransverse zero this is not enough: it forgets the unused equation
directions in $\operatorname{coker}(D_xf)$. Moreover, kernels and cokernels
can jump separately in families even when the morphism $D_xf$ varies
smoothly. The better object is the morphism itself, regarded as a two-term
complex:
\[
  [T_xU\xrightarrow{D_xf}\R].
\]

Our task is to explain why this complex arises intrinsically from the derived
zero locus. The route has four steps. Ordinary smooth derivations identify
the classical notion of linearization. Stable modules provide a setting in
which morphisms, fibers, cofibers, and shifts remain part of one linear
object. The cotangent complex is then characterized as the universal stable
linearization. Finally, its behavior on derived pullbacks produces the
two-term tangent complex of a derived zero locus.

\section{From tangent spaces to stable linearization}
\label[section]{sec:From_Tangent_Spaces_To_Stable_Linearization}

\subsection{Ordinary smooth derivations}
\label[subsection]{sec:Smooth_Derivations}

Let $A$ be an ordinary $\Cinfty$-ring and let $M$ be an ordinary
$A$-module. A map of sets
\[
  D\colon A\longrightarrow M
\]
is a \emph{$\Cinfty$-derivation} if, for every smooth function
$\varphi\colon\R^n\to\R$, it satisfies the smooth chain rule
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:Cinfty_Derivation_Chain_Rule}
  D\bigl(\varphi(a_1,\ldots,a_n)\bigr)
  =
  \sum_{i=1}^n
  (\partial_i\varphi)(a_1,\ldots,a_n)D(a_i).
\end{equation}
Taking $\varphi$ to be addition or multiplication recovers additivity and
the Leibniz rule. Requiring the formula for every smooth $\varphi$ records
the additional operations which distinguish $\Cinfty$-rings from ordinary
commutative $\R$-algebras.

\begin{definition}[Smooth K\"ahler differentials]
  \label[definition]{def:Smooth_Kahler_Differentials}
  The module of \emph{smooth K\"ahler differentials} of $A$ is an
  $A$-module $\Omega_A^1$ equipped with a universal $\Cinfty$-derivation
  \[
    d\colon A\longrightarrow\Omega_A^1.
  \]
  Thus composition with $d$ induces a natural isomorphism
  \[
    \Hom_A(\Omega_A^1,M)
    \cong
    \operatorname{Der}_{\Cinfty}(A,M).
  \]
\end{definition}

The universal property turns a possibly nonlinear algebra into a linear
object. For a free $\Cinfty$-ring, the answer is the familiar module of
differential forms.

\begin{proposition}
  \label[proposition]{prop:Differentials_Free_Cinfty_Ring}
  There is an isomorphism of $\Cinfty(\R^n)$-modules
  \[
    \Omega^1_{\Cinfty(\R^n)}
    \cong
    \bigoplus_{i=1}^n\Cinfty(\R^n)\,dx_i,
  \]
  under which
  \[
    dg
    =
    \sum_{i=1}^n\frac{\partial g}{\partial x_i}\,dx_i.
  \]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
  Given $(m_1,\ldots,m_n)\in M^n$, define
  \[
    D(g)=\sum_i\frac{\partial g}{\partial x_i}m_i.
  \]
  The ordinary chain rule shows that this is a $\Cinfty$-derivation.
  Conversely, \Cref{eq:Cinfty_Derivation_Chain_Rule} forces every derivation
  to have this form, with $m_i=D(x_i)$. Hence derivations into $M$ are
  naturally identified with $M^n$, represented by the displayed free
  module.
\end{proof}

For a manifold $U$, this becomes
\[
  \Omega^1_{\Cinfty(U)}\cong\Gamma(U,T^*U).
\]
Ordinary smooth differentials therefore recover the ordinary cotangent
bundle. At a point $x\in U$, dualizing its fiber recovers $T_xU$.

\subsection{Why ordinary modules are insufficient}
\label[subsection]{sec:Why_Ordinary_Modules_Are_Insufficient}

Now suppose that an ordinary $\Cinfty$-ring has a presentation
\[
  A=B/I,
  \qquad
  B=\Cinfty(\R^n).
\]
The relations contribute their own first-order information. Differentiating
them gives a morphism
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:Conormal_Complex_Heuristic}
  I/I^2
  \longrightarrow
  \Omega_B^1\otimes_BA.
\end{equation}
For a complete-intersection presentation, the correct linearization is the
two-term complex in \Cref{eq:Conormal_Complex_Heuristic}, not merely its
cokernel $\Omega_A^1$. For more complicated presentations, further degrees
can occur.

There is a complementary geometric warning. Consider the family of linear
maps
\[
  \R\xrightarrow{t}\R,
  \qquad t\in\R.
\]
For $t\neq0$, both kernel and cokernel vanish. At $t=0$, both are
one-dimensional. Neither the kernels nor the cokernels form a vector bundle
over the parameter line. The family of morphisms itself, however, varies
perfectly well.

This is exactly what happens for a family of equations. At a zero $x$ of a
section $s\colon U\to E$, taking only $\ker(D_xs)$ retains infinitesimal
solutions but discards the unused equation directions
$\operatorname{coker}(D_xs)$. Taking kernel and cokernel as two unrelated
vector spaces loses the differential which connects them. We therefore seek
a linear category in which
\[
  [T_xU\xrightarrow{D_xs}E_x]
\]
is one object.

\subsection{The stable linear world}
\label[subsection]{sec:Stable_Linear_World}

The guiding analogy is
\[
  \text{ordinary module}
  \quad\rightsquigarrow\quad
  \text{complex of modules}.
\]
Stable homotopy theory provides a homotopy-coherent version of this passage.
We need only its linear formalism, not a construction of its objects.

Write $\mathbb S$ for the sphere, the universal coefficient object of stable
homotopy theory. Stable $\mathbb S$-modules form a stable $\infty$-category.
Its connective objects may be thought of heuristically as
homotopy-coherent abelian groups. Allowing arbitrary shifts gives all stable
$\mathbb S$-modules.

Classical homological algebra lies inside this setting. There is a morphism
of commutative ring spectra
\[
  \mathbb S\longrightarrow H\Z,
\]
and stable $H\Z$-modules are the same as chain complexes of abelian groups,
considered up to quasi-isomorphism. An ordinary abelian group is the special
case concentrated in degree zero.

The distinction between $\mathbb S$ and $H\Z$ is genuine. The positive
homotopy groups of $H\Z$ vanish, while $\mathbb S$ has nontrivial positive
stable homotopy groups. We will not calculate any of them. The point of the
comparison is only that stable linear algebra contains ordinary homological
algebra but is not defined by choosing chain complexes.

For us, four operational properties matter:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item There is a zero object and a direct sum, which is simultaneously a
    product and a coproduct.
  \item Every object has shifts $M[n]$ for all integers $n$.
  \item Every morphism has a fiber and a cofiber, and fiber sequences agree
    with cofiber sequences after shifting.
  \item Tensor products are already derived and preserve cofiber sequences
    in each variable.
\end{enumerate}

These are precisely the operations needed to keep
\Cref{eq:Conormal_Complex_Heuristic} intact and to turn the linearization of a
pushout into an exact triangle.

Let $A$ now be an animated $\Cinfty$-ring. Forgetting the nonpolynomial
smooth operations gives an underlying connective commutative
$\R$-algebra spectrum, which we denote by $A_{\mathrm{alg}}$.

\begin{definition}[Stable module]
  \label[definition]{def:Stable_Module_Animated_Cinfty_Ring}
  A \emph{stable $A$-module} is a module over $A_{\mathrm{alg}}$. We write
  \[
    \operatorname{Mod}_A
    :=
    \operatorname{Mod}_{A_{\mathrm{alg}}}
  \]
  for their stable $\infty$-category.
\end{definition}

If $A$ is an ordinary $\Cinfty$-ring, then
\[
  \operatorname{Mod}_A\simeq D(A),
\]
the derived $\infty$-category of ordinary $A$-modules. Thus an ordinary
$A$-module gives a stable $A$-module concentrated in degree zero. A general
stable $A$-module may occupy several degrees.

For a morphism $A\to B$, extension of scalars is written
\[
  M\longmapsto M\otimes_AB.
\]
This tensor product is formed in stable module categories and is already
derived. The notation
\[
  \Hom_{\operatorname{Mod}_A}(M,N)
\]
denotes the mapping anima.

The direct definition above is intentionally close to the familiar notion
of a module over a ring. There is also an intrinsic construction which starts
only from animated smooth algebras.

\begin{theorem}[Stable-module comparison]
  \label[theorem]{thm:Stable_Module_Comparison}
  There is a natural equivalence of stable $\infty$-categories
  \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:Stable_Module_Comparison_Main}
    \operatorname{Mod}_{A_{\mathrm{alg}}}
    \simeq
    \operatorname{Stab}
    \bigl(
      \operatorname{Alg}_{\Cinfty}(\An)_{/A}
    \bigr).
  \end{equation}
\end{theorem}

Here the stabilization on the right is formed from pointed objects in the
slice. Such a pointed object is a retractive diagram
\[
  A\longrightarrow B\longrightarrow A
\]
whose composite is the identity. Under
\Cref{eq:Stable_Module_Comparison_Main}, a stable $A$-module gives a smooth
square-zero extension of $A$. We return to this intrinsic construction in
\Cref{sec:Supplement_Intrinsic_Stable_Modules}. For the live argument, the
important conclusion is simpler: stable $A$-modules are the correct targets
for smooth derivations once relations and higher information are present.

\section{Square-zero extensions and cotangent complexes}
\label[section]{sec:Square_Zero_Extensions_And_Cotangent_Complexes}

\subsection{Smooth square-zero extensions}
\label[subsection]{sec:Smooth_Square_Zero_Extensions}

We first recall the ordinary construction. Let $A$ be an ordinary
$\Cinfty$-ring and let $M$ be an ordinary $A$-module. The set $A\oplus M$
becomes a $\Cinfty$-ring with square-zero ideal $M$ by the first-order Taylor
formula
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:Smooth_Square_Zero_Formula}
  \varphi_{A\oplus M}
  \bigl((a_1,m_1),\ldots,(a_n,m_n)\bigr)
  =
  \left(
    \varphi_A(a_1,\ldots,a_n),
    \sum_i(\partial_i\varphi)_A(a_1,\ldots,a_n)m_i
  \right).
\end{equation}
The projection $A\oplus M\to A$ is a morphism of $\Cinfty$-rings. A section
of it has the form
\[
  a\longmapsto(a,D(a)).
\]
Formula \Cref{eq:Smooth_Square_Zero_Formula} says precisely that this section
is a morphism of $\Cinfty$-rings if and only if $D$ is a
$\Cinfty$-derivation.

For an animated $\Cinfty$-ring $A$ and a stable $A$-module $M$, the
stable-module comparison supplies a smooth square-zero extension
\[
  A\oplus M\longrightarrow A.
\]
Its underlying animated commutative algebra is the square-zero extension by
the connective cover of $M$:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:Underlying_Module_Smooth_Square_Zero_Extension}
  (A\oplus M)_{\mathrm{alg}}
  \simeq
  A_{\mathrm{alg}}\oplus\tau_{\geq0}M.
\end{equation}
The connective cover appears because an animated algebra is connective,
whereas its stable module category contains arbitrary shifts.

\begin{definition}[Derived smooth derivation]
  \label[definition]{def:Derived_Smooth_Derivation}
  Let $A$ be an animated $\Cinfty$-ring and let $M$ be a stable
  $A$-module. The anima of \emph{smooth derivations} of $A$ with values in
  $M$ is the anima of sections of the square-zero extension:
  \[
    \operatorname{Der}_{\Cinfty}(A,M)
    :=
    \Hom_{
      \operatorname{Alg}_{\Cinfty}(\An)_{/A}}
    (A,A\oplus M).
  \]
\end{definition}

This is the derived version of the elementary correspondence between
derivations and sections. The use of an anima, rather than a set, remembers
homotopies between derivations and all higher coherences.

\subsection{The universal derived cotangent complex}
\label[subsection]{sec:Universal_Derived_Cotangent_Complex}

We can now retain the classical universal property and enlarge only its
linear target category.

\begin{definition}[Cotangent complex]
  \label[definition]{def:Cotangent_Complex_Animated_Cinfty_Ring}
  The \emph{cotangent complex} of an animated $\Cinfty$-ring $A$ is the
  stable $A$-module $\mathbb L_A$ representing smooth derivations. Thus
  there is a natural equivalence of animae
  \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:Cotangent_Complex_Universal_Property}
    \Hom_{\operatorname{Mod}_A}(\mathbb L_A,M)
    \simeq
    \operatorname{Der}_{\Cinfty}(A,M).
  \end{equation}
\end{definition}

For a morphism $A\to B$, the \emph{relative cotangent complex}
$\mathbb L_{B/A}$ similarly represents $A$-linear smooth derivations of $B$.

The definition is worth reading from right to left. A square-zero extension
asks how $A$ may move in an infinitesimal stable direction $M$. The
cotangent complex is the one stable $A$-module from which all such motions
are obtained by mapping out. It is therefore the universal stable
linearization of $A$.

Two adjectives in that sentence matter. The word \emph{stable} allows
relations, relations among relations, and their shifts. The word
\emph{smooth} says that the derivations respect every smooth operation, not
only the polynomial operations in the underlying commutative
$\R$-algebra.

For an ordinary smooth manifold, no higher correction is needed.

For a manifold $U$, we write $\mathbb L_U|_x$ for the fiber at $x$ of the
cotangent complex of its animated function algebra.

\begin{proposition}[Cotangent complex of a manifold]
  \label[proposition]{prop:Cotangent_Complex_Manifold}
  Let $U$ be a manifold and let $x\in U$. Then
  \[
    \mathbb L_U|_x\simeq T_x^*U
  \]
  concentrated in cohomological degree $0$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Idea of proof]
  For $U=\R^n$, the assertion is
  \Cref{prop:Differentials_Free_Cinfty_Ring}. The passage to an open subset
  uses the fact that a smooth localization has vanishing relative cotangent
  complex. The general assertion then follows by locality. We quote that
  localization theorem rather than proving it.
\end{proof}

The proposition recovers the familiar picture at a smooth point. Its value
is that the same universal property also applies to an animated algebra and
can produce a complex with more than one nonzero degree.

\begin{convention}
  \label[convention]{con:Cotangent_Tangent_Cohomological_Grading}
  Cotangent and tangent complexes are indexed cohomologically. A
  quasi-smooth cotangent complex occupies degrees $-1$ and $0$. Its dual
  tangent complex occupies degrees $0$ and $1$.
\end{convention}

The Koszul chain algebras of Talk 5 used a homological grading with
differential lowering degree. These are different presentations of different
objects. A reminder is included in
\Cref{sec:Stable_Linearization_Grading_Conventions}.

\section{Linearizing derived pullbacks}
\label[section]{sec:Linearizing_Derived_Pullbacks}

The cotangent complex becomes calculationally useful because it sends
nonlinear pushout constructions to exact triangles. We use two formal
properties.

\subsection{Base change and transitivity}
\label[subsection]{sec:Cotangent_Base_Change_Transitivity}

\begin{theorem}[Base change]
  \label[theorem]{thm:Cotangent_Complex_Base_Change}
  Suppose that
  \[
  \begin{tikzcd}
    A \rar \dar \drar[pushout] & B \dar \\
    A' \rar & B'
  \end{tikzcd}
  \]
  is a pushout square of animated $\Cinfty$-rings. Then there is a natural
  equivalence of stable $B'$-modules
  \[
    \mathbb L_{B/A}\otimes_BB'
    \simeq
    \mathbb L_{B'/A'}.
  \]
\end{theorem}

Thus relative linearization commutes with extension of scalars across a
pushout.

\begin{theorem}[Transitivity]
  \label[theorem]{thm:Cotangent_Complex_Transitivity}
  For composable morphisms of animated $\Cinfty$-rings
  \[
    A\longrightarrow B\longrightarrow C,
  \]
  there is a natural cofiber sequence of stable $C$-modules
  \[
    \mathbb L_{B/A}\otimes_BC
    \longrightarrow
    \mathbb L_{C/A}
    \longrightarrow
    \mathbb L_{C/B}.
  \]
\end{theorem}

The first term records the linear variation already present in $B$ over
$A$. The last records the new variation introduced by $C$ over $B$.
Transitivity assembles them into the total linearization. The use of a
cofiber sequence, rather than a short exact sequence, is exactly why stable
modules were introduced.

We quote both theorems. Their proofs follow formally from the universal
property of relative cotangent complexes. The rest of the main argument is a
calculation with these two results.

\subsection{The tangent complex of a derived pullback}
\label[subsection]{sec:Linearizing_Derived_Fiber_Product}

Consider smooth maps of manifolds
\[
  X\xrightarrow{f}Z\xleftarrow{g}Y
\]
and form their pullback
\[
  P=X\times_ZY
\]
in the $\infty$-category of derived manifolds. Let $p\in P$ correspond to
$x\in X$ and $y\in Y$ with common image $z\in Z$.

The defining pullback becomes a pushout of animated $\Cinfty$-rings. Base
change and transitivity give a cofiber sequence at $p$:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:Cotangent_Triangle_Derived_Pullback}
  T_z^*Z
  \xrightarrow{((D_xf)^*,-(D_yg)^*)}
  T_x^*X\oplus T_y^*Y
  \longrightarrow
  \mathbb L_P|_p.
\end{equation}
The algebraic derivation of this sequence is recorded in
\Cref{sec:Supplement_Pullback_Cotangent_Triangle}.

The first two terms lie in degree $0$. Their cofiber can therefore be
displayed as
\[
  \mathbb L_P|_p
  \simeq
  [T_z^*Z\longrightarrow T_x^*X\oplus T_y^*Y]
\]
in cohomological degrees $-1$ and $0$. These vector spaces are
finite-dimensional, so the complex is dualizable.

\begin{definition}[Tangent complex at a point]
  \label[definition]{def:Tangent_Complex_At_A_Point}
  If the fiber $\mathbb L_P|_p$ is dualizable, the \emph{tangent complex} of
  $P$ at $p$ is
  \[
    \mathbb T_pP
    :=
    (\mathbb L_P|_p)^\vee.
  \]
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Tangent complex of a derived pullback]
  \label[theorem]{thm:Tangent_Complex_Derived_Pullback}
  With the notation above,
  \[
    \mathbb T_pP
    \simeq
    [
      T_xX\oplus T_yY
      \xrightarrow{D_xf-D_yg}
      T_zZ
    ]
  \]
  in cohomological degrees $0$ and $1$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  Dualizing \Cref{eq:Cotangent_Triangle_Derived_Pullback} turns its cofiber
  sequence into a fiber sequence. The resulting fiber is represented by the
  displayed two-term cochain complex.
\end{proof}

This is the promised connection between derived pullbacks and
deformation-obstruction complexes. The pullback is first formed as a
nonlinear derived object. The cotangent complex linearizes that same
construction into a cofiber sequence of stable modules. Dualization retains
the linearized matching map as a complex instead of replacing it by its
kernel.

Notice what has not happened. We did not decide in advance that a derived
intersection should carry a two-term complex and attach one by hand. The
complex is forced by the universal linearization of the pullback which
already defined the derived intersection.

\subsection{The vertical derivative of a section}
\label[subsection]{sec:Vertical_Derivative_Section}

Let $q\colon E\to U$ be a finite-rank vector bundle and let
\[
  s\colon U\longrightarrow E
\]
be a section. Its derived zero locus is the pullback
\[
\begin{tikzcd}
  Z^{\mathrm{der}}(s) \rar \dar \drar[pullback]
    & U \dar["s"] \\
  U \rar["0"'] & E.
\end{tikzcd}
\]
We have ordered the two copies of $U$ so that the copy carrying $s$ occurs
first. At a zero $x$, both sections take the value $0_x\in E$. Applying
\Cref{thm:Tangent_Complex_Derived_Pullback} gives
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:Unreduced_Tangent_Complex_Section}
  \mathbb T_xZ^{\mathrm{der}}(s)
  \simeq
  [T_xU\oplus T_xU
    \xrightarrow{d_xs-d_x0}
    T_{0_x}E].
\end{equation}

Along the zero section there is a canonical splitting
\[
  T_{0_x}E\cong T_xU\oplus E_x.
\]
Since $q\circ s=q\circ0=\operatorname{id}_U$, the difference
$d_xs-d_x0$ is vertical.

\begin{definition}[Vertical linearization]
  \label[definition]{def:Vertical_Linearization_Section}
  The \emph{vertical linearization} of $s$ at a zero $x$ is the morphism
  \[
    D_xs\colon T_xU\longrightarrow E_x
  \]
  corresponding to $d_xs-d_x0$ under the identification of $E_x$ with the
  vertical tangent space at $0_x$.
\end{definition}

No connection is needed. At a zero, the difference of the derivatives of
the two sections is canonically vertical. In a local trivialization
$E|_V\cong V\times\R^k$, this is the ordinary derivative of the function
representing $s$.

Under the canonical splitting, the differential in
\Cref{eq:Unreduced_Tangent_Complex_Section} sends
\[
  (v,w)\longmapsto(v-w,D_xs(v)).
\]
After the change of variables $a=v$ and $b=v-w$, the complex becomes the
direct sum of
\[
  [T_xU\xrightarrow{\operatorname{id}}T_xU]
\]
and
\[
  [T_xU\xrightarrow{D_xs}E_x].
\]
The first summand is zero as an object of the stable module category. We have
proved the principal formula of the chapter.

\begin{theorem}[Tangent complex of a derived zero locus]
  \label[theorem]{thm:Tangent_Complex_Derived_Zero_Locus}
  Let $s\colon U\to E$ be a section of a finite-rank vector bundle and let
  $x\in U$ be a zero. Then
  \[
    \mathbb T_xZ^{\mathrm{der}}(s)
    \simeq
    [T_xU\xrightarrow{D_xs}E_x]
  \]
  in cohomological degrees $0$ and $1$.
\end{theorem}

The two terms and their differential have arisen by linearizing the pullback
which defines the derived zero locus. In particular, the tangent complex is
not an auxiliary complex attached after the fact.

\section{Deformations, obstructions, and quasi-smoothness}
\label[section]{sec:Deformations_Obstructions_Quasi_Smoothness}

\subsection{Reading the two-term complex}
\label[subsection]{sec:Deformations_And_Obstructions_Zero_Locus}

The cohomology of the tangent complex has an immediate interpretation.

\begin{definition}[Deformation and obstruction spaces]
  \label[definition]{def:Deformation_Obstruction_Spaces}
  Let $x$ be a zero of $s$. Its \emph{deformation space} and
  \emph{obstruction space} are
  \begin{align*}
    \operatorname{Def}_x(s)
    &=H^0\bigl(\mathbb T_xZ^{\mathrm{der}}(s)\bigr)
      =\ker(D_xs),\\
    \operatorname{Obs}_x(s)
    &=H^1\bigl(\mathbb T_xZ^{\mathrm{der}}(s)\bigr)
      =\operatorname{coker}(D_xs).
  \end{align*}
\end{definition}

A tangent vector $v\in T_xU$ is an infinitesimal deformation through
solutions only if the linearized equation vanishes:
\[
  D_xs(v)=0.
\]
The cokernel measures the equation directions which cannot be produced by
linearizing a variation of $x$. It is the first obstruction space.

The terminology should not be overinterpreted. The tangent complex is the
first-order approximation to the full derived object. An element of
$\ker(D_xs)$ need not integrate to an actual curve of solutions, and a
vanishing first obstruction does not by itself solve every nonlinear
extension problem. What the complex does provide is the intrinsic linear
home for both kinds of information.

We now revisit the three calculations from Talk 5.

\begin{example}[A transverse equation]
  \label[example]{ex:Tangent_Complex_Transverse_Equation}
  For $f(x)=x$, the derivative at zero is the identity, so
  \[
    \mathbb T_0Z^{\mathrm{der}}(x)
    \simeq
    [\R\xrightarrow{1}\R].
  \]
  This complex is zero in the stable module category. There are no nonzero
  infinitesimal deformations and no obstructions, as expected for an
  isolated transverse zero.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[A singular but discrete equation]
  \label[example]{ex:Tangent_Complex_Square_Equation}
  For $f(x)=x^2$, the derivative at zero vanishes. Hence
  \[
    \mathbb T_0Z^{\mathrm{der}}(x^2)
    \simeq
    [\R\xrightarrow{0}\R],
  \]
  and
  \[
    \operatorname{Def}_0(x^2)\cong\R,
    \qquad
    \operatorname{Obs}_0(x^2)\cong\R.
  \]
  Talk 5 found that the animated function algebra of this zero locus is
  discrete. This is not a contradiction. Positive homotopy of the function
  algebra and positive-degree cohomology of the tangent complex are distinct
  invariants. A discrete singular ring can have a nontrivial cotangent
  complex.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[A derived self-intersection]
  \label[example]{ex:Tangent_Complex_Zero_Section}
  For the zero section of $U\times\R\to U$, the vertical linearization
  vanishes. Therefore
  \[
    \mathbb T_xZ^{\mathrm{der}}(0)
    \simeq
    [T_xU\xrightarrow{0}\R].
  \]
  Its deformation space is $T_xU$, and its obstruction space is $\R$.
  This time Talk 5 also found a nonzero degree-one homotopy group in the
  animated function algebra. The two phenomena coexist, but they remain
  conceptually different.
\end{example}

The tangent complex is not a complete invariant. The equations $x^2$ and
$x^3$ have the same derivative at zero and hence the same tangent complex,
but their classical truncations are different:
\[
  \frac{\Cinfty(\R)}{(x^2)}
  \not\cong
  \frac{\Cinfty(\R)}{(x^3)}.
\]
Stable linearization retains more than an ordinary tangent space, but less
than the full nonlinear derived zero locus.

\subsection{Transversality and virtual dimension}
\label[subsection]{sec:Transversality_Virtual_Dimension}

For a section $s\colon U\to E$, transversality to the zero section at $x$
means that
\[
  D_xs\colon T_xU\longrightarrow E_x
\]
is surjective. By
\Cref{thm:Tangent_Complex_Derived_Zero_Locus}, this is equivalent to
\[
  H^1\bigl(\mathbb T_xZ^{\mathrm{der}}(s)\bigr)=0.
\]
In that case the tangent complex is concentrated in degree zero and is
equivalent to $\ker(D_xs)$, the ordinary tangent space of the zero manifold.

The same statement holds for a cospan $X\to Z\leftarrow Y$.
Transversality is the surjectivity of
\[
  T_xX\oplus T_yY\longrightarrow T_zZ,
\]
which is precisely the vanishing of the degree-one cohomology of
\Cref{thm:Tangent_Complex_Derived_Pullback}.

This infinitesimal calculation explains the tangent-space part of the
transverse pullback theorem. It does not replace that theorem. Agreement of
the full derived pullback with the ordinary manifold pullback follows from
the universal property studied in Talk 4.

Although the dimensions of deformation and obstruction spaces may jump in a
family, their alternating difference is stable.

\begin{definition}[Virtual dimension]
  \label[definition]{def:Virtual_Dimension_Zero_Locus}
  The \emph{virtual dimension} of a derived zero locus at $x$ is
  \[
    \operatorname{vdim}_xZ^{\mathrm{der}}(s)
    =
    \dim H^0(\mathbb T_xZ^{\mathrm{der}}(s))
    -
    \dim H^1(\mathbb T_xZ^{\mathrm{der}}(s)).
  \]
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}
  \label[proposition]{prop:Virtual_Dimension_Derived_Zero_Locus}
  If $s\colon U\to E$ is a section of a finite-rank vector bundle, then
  \[
    \operatorname{vdim}_xZ^{\mathrm{der}}(s)
    =
    \dim U-\operatorname{rank}E
  \]
  at every zero $x$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
  The tangent complex is $[T_xU\to E_x]$. Rank-nullity gives
  \begin{align*}
    \dim\ker(D_xs)-\dim\operatorname{coker}(D_xs)
    &=\dim T_xU-\dim E_x\\
    &=\dim U-\operatorname{rank}E.
  \end{align*}
\end{proof}

For a derived intersection of manifolds, the same argument gives
\[
  \operatorname{vdim}_p(X\times_ZY)
  =
  \dim X+\dim Y-\dim Z.
\]

The virtual dimension need not equal the dimension of the underlying zero
set. For the zero section of $U\times\R\to U$, the underlying zero set is
$U$, while
\[
  \operatorname{vdim}Z^{\mathrm{der}}(0)=\dim U-1.
\]
The excess normal direction appears as an obstruction direction.

\subsection{The quasi-smooth class}
\label[subsection]{sec:Quasi_Smooth_Affine_Derived_Manifolds}

Derived zero loci of finite-rank vector-bundle sections have perfect
cotangent complexes in degrees $-1$ and $0$. This motivates the finiteness
class which will govern the rest of the seminar.

\begin{definition}[Affine quasi-smoothness]
  \label[definition]{def:Affine_Quasi_Smoothness}
  An affine derived $\Cinfty$-object of finite presentation is
  \emph{quasi-smooth} if its cotangent complex is perfect of Tor-amplitude
  contained in $[-1,0]$.
\end{definition}

Perfection is the finiteness condition which permits dualization and gives
finite-dimensional fibers. The amplitude condition says that the tangent
complex has only a deformation term and a first obstruction term. It does
not say that the object is smooth.

The algebraic definition has a geometric normal form.

\begin{theorem}[Local zero-locus form]
  \label[theorem]{thm:Quasi_Smooth_Local_Zero_Locus_Form}
  Let $X$ be an affine derived $\Cinfty$-object of finite presentation. Then
  $X$ is quasi-smooth if and only if, near every point of its classical
  truncation, it is equivalent to the derived zero locus of a section of a
  finite-rank vector bundle on a manifold.
\end{theorem}

One direction is the tangent-complex calculation of this chapter. The
converse is a derived inverse-function and normal-form theorem, which we do
not prove. It is this converse which turns the amplitude condition from an
abstract homological property into a supply of finite-dimensional local
models.

The theorem also marks the limit of the present affine discussion. To compare
different local zero-locus presentations and glue them into global derived
manifolds, we need a geometric notion of locality. That is the task of the
next talk.

\section{Summary and supplementary materials}
\label[section]{sec:Stable_Linearization_Summary_And_Supplements}

\subsection{Takeaways and next step}
\label[subsection]{sec:Stable_Linearization_Takeaways}

The live route through the chapter can be compressed into seven statements.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Ordinary smooth differentials represent derivations into ordinary
    modules and recover the cotangent bundle of a manifold.
  \item Relations naturally produce complexes, while kernels and cokernels
    separately behave poorly in families.
  \item A stable $A$-module is a module over the underlying commutative ring
    spectrum $A_{\mathrm{alg}}$. Stable modules retain morphisms, shifts,
    fibers, and cofibers as linear data.
  \item The cotangent complex $\mathbb L_A$ represents smooth derivations into
    stable $A$-modules.
  \item Base change and transitivity turn a derived pullback into the tangent
    complex
    \[
      [T_xX\oplus T_yY\longrightarrow T_zZ].
    \]
  \item A vector-bundle zero locus has tangent complex
    \[
      [T_xU\xrightarrow{D_xs}E_x],
    \]
    whose cohomology gives deformation and obstruction spaces.
  \item Quasi-smooth derived objects are the finite-presentation objects
    locally governed by these two-term complexes and zero-locus
    presentations.
\end{enumerate}

We have reached an intrinsic answer to the first-order question. What remains
is to understand the word ``locally'' and the nonuniqueness of the resulting
finite-dimensional presentations.

\begin{question}
  \label[question]{question:From_Affine_Linearization_To_Kuranishi_Charts}
  How does an affine derived calculation acquire open restrictions and a
  structure sheaf, and when do two triples $(U,E,s)$ present the same local
  derived manifold?
\end{question}

Talk 8 answers this by passing from affine animated algebras to local derived
geometry and then interpreting Kuranishi charts as presentations rather than
as intrinsic objects.

\subsection{Supplement: the intrinsic stabilization}
\label[subsection]{sec:Supplement_Intrinsic_Stable_Modules}

This subsection gives a more formal account of stable modules and square-zero
extensions. It is not part of the default live route.

The stable $\infty$-category of stable $\mathbb S$-modules is usually denoted
$\operatorname{Sp}$. The sphere is its tensor unit, so
\[
  \operatorname{Mod}_{\mathbb S}\simeq\operatorname{Sp}.
\]
For an ordinary commutative ring $R$, the Eilenberg--MacLane construction
gives a commutative ring spectrum $HR$ and an equivalence
\[
  \operatorname{Mod}_{HR}\simeq D(R).
\]
Consequently,
\[
  \operatorname{Mod}_{H\Z}\simeq D(\Z).
\]
This distinguishes three levels:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item An ordinary abelian group is an object of $\operatorname{Ab}$.
  \item A chain complex of abelian groups determines an object of
    $D(\Z)\simeq\operatorname{Mod}_{H\Z}$.
  \item A stable $\mathbb S$-module is an object of $\operatorname{Sp}$ and
    need not admit an $H\Z$-module structure.
\end{enumerate}

Now let $A$ be an animated $\Cinfty$-ring. Forgetting the nonpolynomial
smooth operations gives the connective commutative $\R$-algebra spectrum
$A_{\mathrm{alg}}$. Our direct definition was
\[
  \operatorname{Mod}_A
  =
  \operatorname{Mod}_{A_{\mathrm{alg}}}.
\]

There is an intrinsic route to the same linear category. Start with the slice
\[
  \operatorname{Alg}_{\Cinfty}(\An)_{/A}.
\]
Its pointed objects are the retractive diagrams
\[
  A\longrightarrow B\longrightarrow A
\]
whose composite is the identity. Stabilizing means taking coherently
deloopable objects in this pointed setting. Informally, these are the
additive infinitesimal directions at $A$ inside animated smooth algebra.

The stable-module comparison identifies this intrinsic stabilization with
modules over the underlying algebra:
\[
  \operatorname{Stab}
  \bigl(
    \operatorname{Alg}_{\Cinfty}(\An)_{/A}
  \bigr)
  \simeq
  \operatorname{Mod}_{A_{\mathrm{alg}}}.
\]
Under this equivalence, the infinite-loop-object functor gives the smooth
square-zero extension $A\oplus M\to A$. The underlying connective algebra
sees $A_{\mathrm{alg}}\oplus\tau_{\geq0}M$, as in
\Cref{eq:Underlying_Module_Smooth_Square_Zero_Extension}.

This comparison explains why the direct module definition does not discard
the smooth operations. Those operations determine the square-zero extension
inside animated $\Cinfty$-rings and hence determine which sections count as
smooth derivations. The underlying stable module category and the smooth
derivation functor play different roles.

\subsection{Supplement: the pullback cotangent sequence}
\label[subsection]{sec:Supplement_Pullback_Cotangent_Triangle}

We spell out the algebra behind
\Cref{eq:Cotangent_Triangle_Derived_Pullback}. Set
\[
  A=\Cinfty(Z),
  \qquad
  B=\Cinfty(X),
  \qquad
  C=\Cinfty(Y),
\]
and
\[
  D=B\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}^{\mathbb L}_A}C.
\]
There is a natural cofiber sequence of stable $D$-modules
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:Algebraic_Pushout_Cotangent_Sequence}
  \mathbb L_A\otimes_AD
  \longrightarrow
  (\mathbb L_B\otimes_BD)
  \oplus
  (\mathbb L_C\otimes_CD)
  \longrightarrow
  \mathbb L_D.
\end{equation}
The first morphism is induced by the two structure morphisms from $A$, with a
sign on one component.

To obtain \Cref{eq:Algebraic_Pushout_Cotangent_Sequence}, apply transitivity
to $A\to B\to D$ and $A\to C\to D$. Base change identifies
\[
  \mathbb L_{D/B}
  \simeq
  \mathbb L_{C/A}\otimes_CD,
  \qquad
  \mathbb L_{D/C}
  \simeq
  \mathbb L_{B/A}\otimes_BD.
\]
Comparing the two resulting cofiber sequences gives the displayed sequence.

Now extend scalars along the point $p\colon D\to\R$. By
\Cref{prop:Cotangent_Complex_Manifold}, the first two terms become
\[
  T_z^*Z
  \longrightarrow
  T_x^*X\oplus T_y^*Y.
\]
Functoriality identifies the morphism as
$((D_xf)^*,-(D_yg)^*)$. This recovers
\Cref{eq:Cotangent_Triangle_Derived_Pullback}.

\subsection{Supplement: grading conventions}
\label[subsection]{sec:Stable_Linearization_Grading_Conventions}

Two grading conventions occur in the manuscript.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item The Koszul presentations in Talk 5 are nonnegatively graded chain
    algebras whose differentials lower degree. Their homology groups $H_i$
    present the homotopy groups $\pi_i$ of animated function algebras.
  \item Cotangent and tangent complexes are written cohomologically. A
    quasi-smooth cotangent complex occupies degrees $-1$ and $0$, while its
    dual tangent complex occupies degrees $0$ and $1$.
\end{enumerate}

The degree-one Koszul class of a zero section and the degree-one obstruction
space of its tangent complex therefore belong to different objects and have
different meanings. The first is nonlinear function-algebra data. The second
is cohomology of the stable linearization at a point.

Different references make different choices. Spivak writes cotangent
complexes homologically. Steffens and Pascarella also require care when
moving between chain presentations, stable modules, and deformation
complexes. Every displayed tangent complex in this manuscript follows
\Cref{con:Cotangent_Tangent_Cohomological_Grading}.

\subsection{Related literature}
\label[subsection]{sec:Stable_Linearization_Related_Literature}

Steffens develops stable modules by parametrized stabilization and compares
them with modules over the underlying commutative algebra in Section~4.3
\cite[Section~4.3]{Steffens_Derived_Differential_Geometry}. His
Construction~5.1.0.1, Definition~5.1.0.2, and Remark~5.1.0.4 construct the
cotangent complex and record base change and transitivity
\cite[Construction~5.1.0.1, Definition~5.1.0.2, and
Remark~5.1.0.4]{Steffens_Derived_Differential_Geometry}. The manifold
calculation uses his localization results in Corollaries~5.1.1.17--5.1.1.19
\cite[Corollaries~5.1.1.17--5.1.1.19]{Steffens_Derived_Differential_Geometry}.

The affine zero-locus normal form is Steffens,
Proposition~5.1.1.11 and Corollary~5.1.1.13, while virtual dimension is
treated in Definition~5.1.1.20 and Proposition~5.1.1.21
\cite[Proposition~5.1.1.11, Corollary~5.1.1.13,
Definition~5.1.1.20, and
Proposition~5.1.1.21]{Steffens_Derived_Differential_Geometry}.

Pascarella gives a concise account of stable modules, square-zero
extensions, cotangent complexes, and the comparison between the algebraic
and intrinsic viewpoints
\cite[Sections~3.2--3.3 and Appendix~D.3]{Pascarella_Cotangent_Complex_Derived_Differential_Geometry}.
His thesis is especially useful as supplementary reading because it isolates
the precise formalism needed here without requiring the whole theory of
derived manifolds.

The general theory of stable $\infty$-categories, modules over commutative
ring spectra, and tangent categories is developed in Lurie,
\emph{Higher algebra}, especially Sections~1.4 and~7.3
\cite[Sections~1.4 and~7.3]{lurie2016HA}.

Spivak's Section~7 gives an earlier construction and calculation of cotangent
complexes for derived smooth manifolds. Corollaries~7.3--7.6 identify the
cotangent complex of a manifold, establish its two-term amplitude for derived
zero loci, and define virtual dimension
\cite[Section~7, especially Corollaries~7.3--7.6]{Spivak_Derived_Smooth_Manifolds}.
The foundational presentation differs from the one used here, but the
geometric calculation is the same.
