\section{Transverse fiber products}
\label[section]{sec:Transverse_Fiber_Products}

Set-theoretically, the answer is the same in both panels of
\Cref{fig:Two_Intersections}: the intersection consists only of the origin.
Geometrically, the two answers behave very differently. The transverse
intersection is stable locally under sufficiently small perturbations. For the
tangent intersection, replace the parabola by
\[
  y=x^2+\varepsilon.
\]
Its intersection with the $x$-axis consists of two points when
$\varepsilon<0$, one point when $\varepsilon=0$, and no points when
$\varepsilon>0$. For the fixed equation at $\varepsilon=0$, we will find an
infinitesimal solution direction which cannot extend to an actual curve of
solutions. This is distinct from varying the equation: if $\varepsilon$ is
allowed to vary, then $x(\tau)=\tau$ and $\varepsilon(\tau)=-\tau^2$ do give
a curve of solutions in the total family.

\begin{question*}
  What geometric object should replace a nontransverse intersection so that it
  remembers the defining equations, agrees with the ordinary intersection in
  the transverse case, and behaves well in families?
\end{question*}

Derived manifolds provide such an object. Transversality gives the first
test for the construction: a derived intersection must recover the ordinary
manifold when the derivatives are independent. The nontransverse examples
will show what additional information is needed.
The same intersection-theoretic problem is the starting point of Spivak's
construction of derived smooth manifolds
\cite[Sections~1--3]{Spivak_Derived_Smooth_Manifolds}.

Let $f\colon X\to Z$ and $g\colon Y\to Z$ be smooth maps between smooth
manifolds. Their fiber product as sets is
\[
  X\times_ZY
  =\{(x,y)\in X\times Y\mid f(x)=g(y)\}.
\]
Without an additional hypothesis, this subset of $X\times Y$ need not be a
smooth manifold of the expected dimension. Transversality is the hypothesis
which rules out this defect.

\begin{definition}[Transverse maps]\label[definition]{def:Transverse_Maps}
  Let $f\colon X\to Z$ and $g\colon Y\to Z$ be smooth maps. For
  $(x,y)\in X\times_ZY$, with common image $z=f(x)=g(y)$, define the
  \emph{linearized matching map}
  \[
    \delta_{x,y}
    =D_xf-D_yg
    \colon T_xX\oplus T_yY\longrightarrow T_zZ.
  \]
  We say that $f$ and $g$ are \emph{transverse} if $\delta_{x,y}$ is
  surjective for every $(x,y)\in X\times_ZY$.
\end{definition}

Equivalently, $f$ and $g$ are transverse if
\[
  \operatorname{im}(D_xf)+\operatorname{im}(D_yg)=T_zZ
\]
whenever $f(x)=g(y)=z$. In particular, if $A,B\subseteq Z$ are embedded
submanifolds and $f$ and $g$ are their inclusions, this condition becomes
\[
  T_zA+T_zB=T_zZ
\]
at every $z\in A\cap B$.

We will use the following standard consequence of the regular value theorem.

\begin{proposition}[Transverse preimages]
  \label[proposition]{prop:Transverse_Preimages}
  Let $h\colon M\to N$ be a smooth map and let $S\subseteq N$ be an embedded
  submanifold. Suppose that
  \[
    \operatorname{im}(D_xh)+T_{h(x)}S=T_{h(x)}N
  \]
  for every $x\in h^{-1}(S)$. Then $h^{-1}(S)$ is an embedded submanifold of
  $M$ of codimension $\operatorname{codim}_N(S)$, and
  \[
    T_xh^{-1}(S)=(D_xh)^{-1}(T_{h(x)}S)
  \]
  for every $x\in h^{-1}(S)$.
\end{proposition}

This result is also called the transverse preimage theorem; see
\cite[Chapter~8]{lee2000smooth}. We take it as recalled differential topology
and use it to prove the principal result of this section.

\begin{theorem}[Transverse fiber product theorem]
  \label[theorem]{thm:Transverse_Fiber_Product}
  Let $f\colon X\to Z$ and $g\colon Y\to Z$ be transverse smooth maps. Then
  $X\times_ZY$ is a smooth manifold. If it is nonempty, its dimension is
  \[
    \dim(X\times_ZY)=\dim X+\dim Y-\dim Z.
  \]
  For every $(x,y)\in X\times_ZY$, there is a natural isomorphism
  \[
    T_{(x,y)}(X\times_ZY)
    \cong
    \ker\bigl(\delta_{x,y}\colon T_xX\oplus T_yY\to T_zZ\bigr).
  \]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  Consider the product map
  \[
    h=f\times g\colon X\times Y\longrightarrow Z\times Z,
    \qquad
    h(x,y)=(f(x),g(y)).
  \]
  If $\Delta_Z\subseteq Z\times Z$ denotes the diagonal, then
  \[
    X\times_ZY=h^{-1}(\Delta_Z).
  \]
  At a point $(z,z)\in\Delta_Z$, its tangent space is
  \[
    T_{(z,z)}\Delta_Z=\{(w,w)\mid w\in T_zZ\}.
  \]
  The linear map
  \[
    \pi_z\colon T_zZ\oplus T_zZ\longrightarrow T_zZ,
    \qquad
    \pi_z(a,b)=a-b,
  \]
  is surjective and has kernel $T_{(z,z)}\Delta_Z$. Consequently, $h$ is
  transverse to $\Delta_Z$ at $(x,y)$ if and only if the composite
  \[
    T_xX\oplus T_yY
    \xrightarrow{D_{(x,y)}h}
    T_zZ\oplus T_zZ
    \xrightarrow{\pi_z}
    T_zZ
  \]
  is surjective. Here we use the elementary fact that a linear map to a vector
  space $W$ is transverse to a subspace $U\subseteq W$ if and only if its
  composite with $W\to W/U$ is surjective. The displayed composite is
  precisely
  \[
    (u,v)\longmapsto D_xf(u)-D_yg(v)=\delta_{x,y}(u,v).
  \]
  Thus the transversality of $f$ and $g$ is equivalent to the transversality
  of $h$ to $\Delta_Z$.

  By \Cref{prop:Transverse_Preimages}, the inverse image
  $h^{-1}(\Delta_Z)$ is a smooth submanifold of $X\times Y$. Since the diagonal
  has codimension $\dim Z$ in $Z\times Z$, this proves the dimension formula.

  The tangent-space statement in \Cref{prop:Transverse_Preimages} gives
  \begin{align*}
    T_{(x,y)}h^{-1}(\Delta_Z)
    &\cong
      \{(u,v)\in T_xX\oplus T_yY
        \mid (D_xf(u),D_yg(v))\in T_{(z,z)}\Delta_Z\} \\
    &=\{(u,v)\in T_xX\oplus T_yY
        \mid D_xf(u)=D_yg(v)\} \\
    &=\ker(\delta_{x,y}),
  \end{align*}
  as asserted.

  Finally, suppose a manifold $W$ has smooth maps to $X$ and $Y$ whose
  composites to $Z$ agree. Their product is a smooth map to $X\times Y$
  with image in the embedded submanifold $h^{-1}(\Delta_Z)$, so its
  corestriction is smooth. It is the unique map compatible with the two
  projections. Thus the constructed manifold is the categorical pullback
  in manifolds.
\end{proof}

In particular, under the hypotheses of
\Cref{thm:Transverse_Fiber_Product}, there is a short exact sequence
\[
  0\longrightarrow T_{(x,y)}(X\times_ZY)
  \longrightarrow T_xX\oplus T_yY
  \xrightarrow{\delta_{x,y}}T_zZ
  \longrightarrow0.
\]
The question motivating the following examples is what remains meaningful when
the last map is no longer surjective.

\section{Nontransverse fiber products}
\label[section]{sec:Nontransverse_Fiber_Products}

For a transverse intersection, the kernel of the matching map is the tangent
space of the intersection. Without transversality, this kernel still describes
solutions of the linearized matching equation, but these need not extend to
actual solutions. Attempting to extend a first-order solution to second order
produces an error. In local coordinates, changing the second-order correction
changes this error by an element of the image of the matching map. The
remaining obstruction therefore takes values in its cokernel. We will see
this explicitly in \Cref{ex:Tangent_Line_And_Parabola}.

This gives a role to both the kernel, which describes first-order
solutions, and the cokernel, in which obstruction classes take values.
We package the matching map into a two-term complex.

\begin{notation}\label[notation]{not:Linearized_Matching_Complex}
  For arbitrary smooth maps $f\colon X\to Z$ and $g\colon Y\to Z$, and a point
  $(x,y)\in X\times_ZY$, write
  \[
    \mathbb{T}_{x,y}(f,g)
    =\bigl[T_xX\oplus T_yY\xrightarrow{\delta_{x,y}}T_zZ\bigr],
  \]
  with the source in homological degree $0$ and the target in degree $-1$.
  We call this the \emph{linearized matching complex}. Thus
  \[
    H_0\mathbb{T}_{x,y}(f,g)=\ker(\delta_{x,y}),
    \qquad
    H_{-1}\mathbb{T}_{x,y}(f,g)=\operatorname{coker}(\delta_{x,y}).
  \]
\end{notation}

For now the complex is a way to keep the linear map together with its kernel
and cokernel. \Cref{chap:Stable_Linearization_And_Tangent_Complexes} will identify it with the tangent complex of the
derived intersection. The calculations here require only linear algebra.

We now return to the two intersections pictured in
\Cref{fig:Two_Intersections}. The following two examples calculate their
linearized matching complexes. In both cases the set-theoretic intersection
consists only of the origin, but the complexes are different.

\begin{example}[Two transverse axes]\label[example]{ex:Transverse_Axes}
  Parametrize the two coordinate axes by
  \[
    i\colon\R\longrightarrow\R^2,
    \quad i(s)=(s,0),
    \qquad
    k\colon\R\longrightarrow\R^2,
    \quad k(t)=(0,t).
  \]
  Their intersection consists of the origin. The linearized matching map is
  \[
    \delta_{0,0}\colon\R^2\longrightarrow\R^2,
    \qquad
    \delta_{0,0}(u,v)=(u,-v).
  \]
  It is an isomorphism, so the maps are transverse and
  \[
    H_0\mathbb{T}_{0,0}(i,k)=0,
    \qquad
    H_{-1}\mathbb{T}_{0,0}(i,k)=0.
  \]
\end{example}

\begin{example}[A tangent line and parabola]
  \label[example]{ex:Tangent_Line_And_Parabola}
  Parametrize the $x$-axis and the parabola $y=x^2$ by
  \[
    i\colon\R\longrightarrow\R^2,
    \quad i(s)=(s,0),
    \qquad
    j\colon\R\longrightarrow\R^2,
    \quad j(t)=(t,t^2).
  \]
  A point $(s,t)$ lies in the fiber product precisely when
  \[
    s=t,
    \qquad
    t^2=0.
  \]
  Thus the underlying fiber product is again a single point. At this point,
  however, the linearized matching map is
  \[
    \delta_{0,0}\colon\R^2\longrightarrow\R^2,
    \qquad
    \delta_{0,0}(u,v)=(u-v,0).
  \]
  It follows that
  \[
    H_0\mathbb{T}_{0,0}(i,j)
    =\{(u,u)\mid u\in\R\}\cong\R
  \]
  and
  \[
    H_{-1}\mathbb{T}_{0,0}(i,j)
    =\R^2/(\R\times\{0\})\cong\R.
  \]
  We refer provisionally to these as the infinitesimal deformation space and
  the obstruction space. Their alternating dimension is zero, which agrees
  with the expected dimension of the intersection.
\end{example}

The class represented by $(1,1)$ in degree $0$ is a solution of the linearized
matching equation. It is not a tangent vector to the ordinary intersection,
which is a single point. To see why this first-order solution cannot extend
to second order, write the matching equation as
\[
  F(s,t)=i(s)-j(t)=(s-t,-t^2)=0.
\]
An extension with initial velocity $(1,1)$ would have expansions
\[
  s(\tau)=\tau+a\tau^2+O(\tau^3),
  \qquad
  t(\tau)=\tau+b\tau^2+O(\tau^3).
\]
Substitution gives
\[
  F(s(\tau),t(\tau))=\tau^2(a-b,-1)+O(\tau^3).
\]
The quadratic error is therefore
\[
  (a-b,-1)=(0,-1)+\delta_{0,0}(a,b).
\]
Changing the second-order correction changes this error by an element of
$\operatorname{im}(\delta_{0,0})$. Its class
\[
  [(0,-1)]\in\operatorname{coker}(\delta_{0,0})
  =H_{-1}\mathbb{T}_{0,0}(i,j)
\]
is nonzero, so no choice of $a,b$ makes the equation hold through second
order. In particular, $(1,1)$ cannot be the velocity of an actual curve of
solutions.

The cokernel is the space in which this obstruction takes values; the
obstruction class itself uses the quadratic term of $F$. It is not determined
by the linearized complex alone. A nonzero cokernel does not by itself imply
that a given first-order solution is obstructed.

The same example can be presented as a zero locus in one variable. Eliminating
$s$ gives the smooth function
\[
  q\colon\R\longrightarrow\R,
  \qquad
  q(t)=t^2.
\]
Its linearized complex at the origin is
\[
  [\R\xrightarrow{D_0q}\R]
  =
  [\R\xrightarrow{0}\R].
\]
We can compare this directly with the matching complex in
\Cref{ex:Tangent_Line_And_Parabola}. Let
\[
  a=(1,1),
  \qquad
  b=(1,0)
\]
be a basis of the source of $\delta_{0,0}$, and let $e_1,e_2$ be the standard
basis of its target. Then
\[
  \delta_{0,0}(a)=0,
  \qquad
  \delta_{0,0}(b)=e_1.
\]
Consequently, the matching complex splits as
\[
  \mathbb{T}_{0,0}(i,j)
  \cong
  [\R b\iso\R e_1]
  \oplus
  [\R a\xrightarrow{0}\R e_2].
\]
The first summand is acyclic, while the second is isomorphic to the linearized
complex of $q$.

This illustrates a change of presentation which should preserve the geometric
object. Adding a variable $s$ and an equation $s=0$ to $q(t)=0$ adds an
acyclic summand $[\R\xrightarrow{1}\R]$ to its linearized complex: the new
variable is uniquely eliminated by the new equation. In contrast, appending
the equation $0=0$ without adding a variable contributes $[0\to\R]$.
Although the zero set is unchanged in both cases, only the first operation
preserves the linearized deformation and obstruction spaces.

Already the equations $t=0$ and $t^2=0$ have the same zero set but different
linearized complexes, respectively $[\R\xrightarrow{1}\R]$ and
$[\R\xrightarrow{0}\R]$. Passing to the zero set therefore forgets
information visible in the derivative. The next example shows that the
derivative alone is still insufficient.

\begin{example}[The same tangent complex from different equations]
\label[example]{ex:Square_And_Cube_Infinitesimal_Tests}
The equations $x^2=0$ and $x^3=0$ both have zero set $\{0\}$ and
linearized complex $[\R\xrightarrow0\R]$. Nevertheless their first-order
solutions have different extension behavior. For an attempted extension
\[
  x(\tau)=\tau+a\tau^2+O(\tau^3),
\]
we obtain
\[
  x(\tau)^2=\tau^2+O(\tau^3),
  \qquad
  x(\tau)^3=\tau^3+O(\tau^4).
\]
No choice of $a$ makes the square vanish through second order, whereas the
cubic does vanish through second order. Thus the first-order solution extends
one order further for the cubic, although neither equation admits an actual
curve of solutions with initial velocity $1$.
The nonlinear equation contains information beyond its tangent complex.
\end{example}

These examples show why the nonlinear equations matter, even when we know
their zero set and their linearization. The next example raises a different
issue: an equation can contribute to the linearized problem even when it
imposes no condition at all on the set of solutions.

\begin{example}[An excess self-intersection]
\label[example]{ex:Excess_Self_Intersection}
Compare an ordinary point with the self-intersection of the origin in $\R$,
given by two maps $*\to\R$. The point has no variables and no equations.
The self-intersection has no variables and one matching equation, which
happens to vanish. Its ordinary pullback is again a point, but the two
linearized complexes are respectively
\[
  [0\to0]\qquad\text{and}\qquad[0\to\R].
\]
The second has alternating dimension $-1$, which cannot be the dimension
of an ordinary manifold. There is no nonzero first-order solution direction
to obstruct. Rather, perturbing one of the two points away from the other
produces a matching error which cannot be corrected, since there are no
variables.

We therefore seek a geometric object which remembers this matching equation,
even though it imposes no condition on the set of solutions. The derived
self-intersection will be such an object. Its linearized complex will be
$[0\to\R]$, distinguishing it from an ordinary point. We will construct it in
\Cref{chap:Derived_Zero_Loci_And_Tangent_Complexes}.
\end{example}

The preceding examples concern fixed equations. A one-parameter family shows
why it is useful to work with the entire two-term complex rather than only its
kernel or cokernel.

\begin{example}[A family with jumping kernels]
  \label[example]{ex:Family_With_Jumping_Kernels}
  For $t\in\R$, consider the linear map
  \[
    L_t\colon\R\longrightarrow\R,
    \qquad
    L_t(u)=tu.
  \]
  Its kernel and cokernel are
  \[
    \ker L_t=
    \begin{cases}
      0,&t\neq0,\\
      \R,&t=0,
    \end{cases}
    \qquad
    \operatorname{coker}L_t=
    \begin{cases}
      0,&t\neq0,\\
      \R,&t=0.
    \end{cases}
  \]
  Neither the kernels nor the cokernels form a vector bundle of constant rank
  over the parameter line.

  Let $E_0=E_{-1}=\R\times\R$ be trivial line bundles over the first
  factor and define a bundle map
  \[
    d\colon E_0\longrightarrow E_{-1},
    \qquad
    d(t,u)=(t,tu).
  \]
  This gives a smooth two-term complex of vector bundles
  \[
    [E_0\xrightarrow{d}E_{-1}]
  \]
  whose fiber over $t$ is $[\R\xrightarrow{L_t}\R]$. The complex varies
  smoothly even though the dimensions of its homology groups jump. The
  alternating dimension
  \[
    \dim\ker L_t-\dim\operatorname{coker}L_t=0
  \]
  is independent of $t$.

  The total set of solutions is
  \[
    \{(t,u)\in\R^2\mid tu=0\},
  \]
  which is the union of the two coordinate axes. The additional branch over
  $t=0$ records the jump in $\ker L_t$, and the two branches meet at the
  singular origin.

  Here $L_t$ linearizes the equation in $u$ at $u=0$, with $t$ held fixed.
  Thus the displayed complex is relative to the parameter $t$. If we instead
  allow both $t$ and $u$ to vary, the derivative of $(t,u)\mapsto tu$ at the
  origin is zero, giving the absolute linearized complex
  $[\R^2\xrightarrow0\R]$ of the total zero locus there.
\end{example}

The vector bundles and their differential give a single smooth object
throughout the family. At each parameter, we take the corresponding linear
map and calculate its kernel and cokernel. These can change dimension even
though the vector bundles have fixed ranks.

We can now collect what the examples require from a replacement of singular
fiber products.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item It should admit arbitrary pullbacks and recover the ordinary smooth
    fiber product when the maps are transverse.
  \item It should describe the nonlinear defining equations in a
    presentation-independent way, not merely their set of solutions or their
    first derivatives.
  \item It should organize deformation and obstruction data in families, even
    when the dimensions of the individual deformation and obstruction spaces
    jump.
\end{enumerate}

The second requirement contains two lessons from the preceding calculations.
The tangent line--parabola intersection and the zero locus of $t^2$ are two
presentations of the same problem, so the desired object should not depend on
the redundant variable. On the other hand, the zero set and the linearized
complex are both too coarse: the first cannot distinguish $t$ from $t^2$, and
the second cannot distinguish $t^2$ from $t^3$.

Derived manifolds will provide a construction satisfying these requirements. It may be described via a universal property: they form a universal
enlargement of manifolds in which finite homotopy limits exist and transverse
pullbacks are preserved. We will explain these terms and state the precise
universal property in
\Cref{chap:From_Smooth_Algebra_To_Derived_Manifolds}. For now, the point is that
there is a systematic construction of intersections, independent of a chosen
system of coordinates or a redundant variable eliminated by an equation.
The next chapters develop the algebraic tools needed to construct these
objects and establish their properties.

\section{Elliptic equations and finite-dimensional reduction}
\label[section]{sec:Elliptic_Moduli}

The final part of the seminar will prove \emph{Steffens's relative elliptic
representability theorem}. This is a principal application of the derived
geometry we will develop: it gives a geometric structure to moduli problems
defined by nonlinear elliptic partial differential equations. Such equations
occur throughout differential geometry. For example, pseudo-holomorphic maps
from a Riemann surface to an almost complex manifold satisfy an elliptic
equation, and the last talk will apply the theorem to their moduli problem.

Informally, Steffens's theorem says that a family of elliptic equations on a
proper family of manifolds has a solution stack represented, relative to its
parameters, by a quasi-smooth derived $\Cinfty$-scheme
\cite[Theorem~3.4.3]{Steffens_Representability_Elliptic_Moduli}.
Properness supplies the compactness needed for elliptic analysis.
Quasi-smoothness means that, locally over the parameters, the solutions admit
presentations as derived zero loci of sections of finite-rank vector bundles
on finite-dimensional manifolds. Thus the finite-dimensional geometry of
equations studied above also describes solutions of certain
infinite-dimensional problems. The theorem asserts representability of the
solution functor, so these local presentations describe an intrinsic moduli
object, including its families and deformation theory.

\Cref{chap:Section_Stacks_Jets_Solution_Stacks} constructs the solution stacks
to which the theorem applies. We state the precise theorem in
\Cref{thm:Relative_Elliptic_Representability} and prove it over
\Cref{chap:Elliptic_Equations_Kuranishi_Models,chap:Elliptic_Representability_Smooth_Derived_Bridge,chap:Elliptic_Representability_Derived_Charts}.
Here we explain the role of finite-dimensional reduction in that proof,
starting with an equation on a fixed manifold.

Let $M$ be a closed smooth manifold, let $\pi\colon V\to M$ be a smooth fiber
bundle, and let $F\to M$ be a smooth vector bundle. Suppose that
\[
  P\colon\Gamma(V)\longrightarrow\Gamma(F)
\]
is a nonlinear elliptic differential operator. We write
\[
  \operatorname{Sol}(P)=P^{-1}(0).
\]
A point of $\operatorname{Sol}(P)$ is a section satisfying the geometric
equation encoded by $P$. For the moment this notation denotes the ordinary
set of solutions. The theorem concerns the derived solution stack, whose
construction must also account for families of solutions and their defining
equations.

For a concrete model, take $V=F=M\times\R$ over a closed connected
Riemannian manifold. The semilinear equation
\[
  P(u)=\Delta u+u^2=0
\]
uses the nonnegative Laplacian $\Delta=d^*d$ and defines a subset
$\operatorname{Sol}(P)\subseteq C^\infty(M)$. It arises
as the critical-point equation for the energy
$\int_M(\tfrac12|du|^2+u^3/3)$. The zero function is a solution and its linearization is
$D_0P=\Delta$. Since $M$ is closed and connected, its kernel consists of the
constant functions, and self-adjointness identifies its cokernel with the same
one-dimensional space.

Nevertheless, the only real solution is $u=0$: integrating the equation gives
\[
  0=\int_M P(u)=\int_M u^2,
\]
since the integral of $\Delta u$ vanishes on a closed manifold. The constant
function $1$ is a first-order solution at zero. To extend it to second order,
try
\[
  u(\tau)=\tau+\tau^2v+O(\tau^3).
\]
The coefficient of $\tau^2$ in $P(u(\tau))$ is $\Delta v+1$. Its integral
is $\operatorname{vol}(M)$, so it cannot vanish for any $v$. Equivalently,
the quadratic error represents the nonzero class of $1$ in
$\operatorname{coker}(\Delta)$. This is the same obstruction mechanism as
for the tangent line and parabola. We will return to this example when we
carry out a finite-dimensional reduction later in the seminar.

Why should such an equation admit the finite-dimensional local presentations
promised by the theorem? Both $\Gamma(V)$ and $\Gamma(F)$ are
infinite-dimensional. Ellipticity first gives a finiteness statement about
the linearized equation; finite-dimensional reduction then extends this
statement to the nonlinear equation near a solution.

Write
\[
  T^{\mathrm{vert}}V
  =\ker(D\pi\colon TV\longrightarrow TM)
\]
for the vertical tangent bundle. At a solution $u$, the tangent space to the
space of sections is
\[
  T_u\Gamma(V)\cong\Gamma(u^*T^{\mathrm{vert}}V).
\]
If $V$ is a vector bundle, then $T^{\mathrm{vert}}V\cong\pi^*V$, and this
reduces to the familiar identification $T_u\Gamma(V)\cong\Gamma(V)$.
The linearization of $P$ at $u$ is therefore the elliptic operator
\[
  D_uP\colon
  \Gamma(u^*T^{\mathrm{vert}}V)
  \longrightarrow
  \Gamma(F).
\]
We therefore obtain the two-term complex
\[
  \mathbb{T}_uP
  =\bigl[
      \Gamma(u^*T^{\mathrm{vert}}V)
      \xrightarrow{D_uP}
      \Gamma(F)
    \bigr]
\]
in homological degrees $0$ and $-1$. Its homology is the infinitesimal
deformation space and obstruction space of the equation at $u$:
\[
  H_0(\mathbb{T}_uP)=\ker(D_uP),
  \qquad
  H_{-1}(\mathbb{T}_uP)=\operatorname{coker}(D_uP).
\]
Ellipticity and compactness imply that these spaces are finite-dimensional.
As $u$ varies, their dimensions can jump, so the complex $\mathbb{T}_uP$ is
again more stable than its homology groups separately. This is the
infinite-dimensional version of \Cref{ex:Family_With_Jumping_Kernels}.\footnote{
  This is the motivating linear picture in
  \cite[Sections~1--2]{Pardon_Representability_Elliptic_Fredholm}.}

The analytic step is to solve the nonlinear equation in the remaining
directions, leaving only finitely many variables and equations. The following
classical reduction previews that step. We quote it here under the usual
analytic hypotheses: suitable Sobolev completions make $P$ a smooth map of
Banach manifolds with Fredholm linearization, and elliptic regularity
identifies its nearby Sobolev solutions with smooth sections. These are the
tools developed in \Cref{chap:Elliptic_Representability_Derived_Charts}.

\begin{fact}[Local finite-dimensional reduction]
  \label[fact]{fact:Finite_Dimensional_Reduction}
  Let $P$ satisfy the analytic hypotheses just stated, and let
  $u\in\operatorname{Sol}(P)$. There are
  finite-dimensional vector spaces $K$ and $C$, an open neighborhood $U$ of
  $0$ in $K$, and a smooth map
  \[
    \kappa\colon U\longrightarrow C,
    \qquad
    \kappa(0)=0,
  \]
  such that a neighborhood of $u$ in $\operatorname{Sol}(P)$ is identified
  with $\kappa^{-1}(0)$. Moreover, at every $v\in\kappa^{-1}(0)$, the
  finite-dimensional complex
  \[
    [K\xrightarrow{D_v\kappa}C]
  \]
  is quasi-isomorphic to the complex $\mathbb{T}_{u_v}P$ at the corresponding
  solution $u_v$.
\end{fact}

Here \emph{quasi-isomorphic} means that the complexes can be connected
by a zigzag of chain maps which induce isomorphisms on $H_0$ and $H_{-1}$. In
particular, they have the same deformation and obstruction spaces.

The mechanism behind the fact is worth keeping in mind. After passing to
Sobolev completions, $D_uP$ is a Fredholm operator. One chooses splittings
which separate its kernel, image, and cokernel. The inverse function theorem
solves the equation in the complementary directions, leaving a
finite-dimensional obstruction map $\kappa$. Elliptic bootstrapping then shows
that the resulting solutions are smooth. We quote this analytic argument; the
precise conclusion above is
\cite[Fact~1.0.0.1]{Steffens_Derived_Differential_Geometry}.

Thus the map $\kappa$ gives a finite-dimensional presentation of the equation
near the chosen solution. Its zero set describes nearby solutions, while its
derivative recovers their deformation and obstruction spaces.
The nonlinear terms of $\kappa$ determine higher-order obstructions,
as in the preceding examples $t\mapsto t^2$ and $t\mapsto t^3$.
This explains why a derived zero locus is a plausible local model for the
solution stack. To prove Steffens's theorem, we must establish this
description for the solution functor itself, compatibly with parameters,
and glue the resulting local models. The formal thickening argument in
\Cref{chap:Elliptic_Representability_Smooth_Derived_Bridge} and the analytic
argument in \Cref{chap:Elliptic_Representability_Derived_Charts} provide these
local derived presentations.

\section{From local zero loci to coherent moduli problems}
\label[section]{sec:Local_Zero_Loci_Not_Enough}

The construction of $\kappa$ depends on choices of Sobolev completions, splittings,
finite-dimensional subspaces, and neighborhoods. Different choices can
produce zero-locus presentations
\[
  \kappa\colon U\longrightarrow C
  \qquad\text{and}\qquad
  \kappa'\colon U'\longrightarrow C'
\]
of different dimensions. An identification of their underlying zero sets says
too little, just as equality of the zero sets of $t$ and $t^2$ says too little.

There are therefore two global problems.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item One must specify when two local finite-dimensional reductions present
    the same geometric object.
  \item One must glue these local objects on overlaps, together with
    compatibilities between the comparison maps and all higher
    compatibilities.
\end{enumerate}

Higher-categorical language records the comparison maps, homotopies between
comparisons, and their iterated compatibilities. Later we will formulate the
equation as a \emph{derived solution functor}, which describes families of
solutions together with additional information about the equations. Its
definition belongs to \Cref{chap:Section_Stacks_Jets_Solution_Stacks}; it is
not simply the assignment of an ordinary solution set to each parameter.
Identifying local finite-dimensional reductions with charts of this functor
is an additional step beyond identifying their ordinary zero sets. If the
functor is representable, its representing object is unique. This is the representability viewpoint
discussed in \cite[Section~1]{Pardon_Representability_Elliptic_Fredholm}.

For elliptic equations, the derived comparison of the local reductions and
descent will show that the solution stack is representable over its parameter
stack by quasi-smooth derived $\Cinfty$-schemes. When the parameter object is
a point, the solution stack itself is a quasi-smooth derived $\Cinfty$-scheme.
Constructing virtual fundamental classes or enumerative invariants then
requires further geometric input, such as orientations and compactness.

\section{Further examples}
\label[section]{sec:Why_Derived_Summary_And_Further_Reading}

An intersection can have an ordinary zero set of the wrong positive
dimension. The zero map $\R^n\to\R^r$ has all of $\R^n$ as its zero set,
while the expected dimension is $n-r$. Its linearized complex
$[\R^n\xrightarrow0\R^r]$ displays both the variables and the redundant
equations. The derived construction will distinguish this presentation
from the manifold $\R^n$ with no equations imposed.

\begin{exercise}
Express the intersection of the graph of $f\colon U\to\R^r$ with the
zero section of $U\times\R^r$ as a fibre product. Separate the acyclic
summand of its linearized matching complex to obtain $[T_xU\to\R^r]$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
For $m\geq1$, find the first order at which the equations $x^m=0$ and
$x^{m+1}=0$ can be distinguished by substituting
$x(\tau)=\tau+O(\tau^2)$. As an algebraic reformulation, find the least
$N$ for which $x=\varepsilon$ satisfies one equation but not the other in
the truncated polynomial ring $\R[\varepsilon]/(\varepsilon^N)$.
\end{exercise}
