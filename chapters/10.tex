\chapter{Section stacks and derived solution stacks}
\label[chapter]{chap:Section_Stacks_Jets_Solution_Stacks}

A differential operator assigns to a function, section, or map an expression
in its derivatives. Its solutions often describe a geometric condition:
harmonicity, stationarity of an energy, or compatibility with a complex
structure. The equation can be defined for smooth families of unknowns.
The resulting functor of solutions is a stack, whose geometric
representability is a separate question.

Our running example is the nonlinear elliptic equation
\[
 P(u)=\Delta u+u^2=0
\]
on a closed connected Riemannian manifold. Its ordinary solution set is
a single point, but its infinitesimal solutions and obstructions are
both nontrivial. The eventual conclusion is concrete: near zero, its
derived solution object is the multiple point defined by $c^2=0$.
\Cref{chap:Elliptic_Representability_Derived_Charts} will prove this by
eliminating the mean-zero part of $u$, leaving its constant part $c$
as the remaining variable.

This chapter sets up the functor that the later analytic argument will
represent. Geometric examples lead to section stacks and their derived
fibres. Jets then express the finite-order condition, and linearization
gives the definition of ellipticity. Each construction is formulated
for families, so it can be used over a parameter stack.

\section{Differential equations from geometry}

The examples below identify the unknowns, their equations, and the
bundles in which the equations take values. We begin with scalar
functions and then consider maps, where the output bundle depends
on the unknown.

\begin{example}[Harmonic functions]
For a Riemannian manifold $N$, the Laplacian is an operator
\[
 \Delta\colon\Cinfty(N)\longrightarrow\Cinfty(N).
\]
We use the nonnegative convention $\Delta=d^*d$.
The equation $\Delta u=0$ says that $u$ is harmonic.
It occurs in potential theory and in extending boundary data into a domain.
On a closed connected manifold, integration by parts shows that harmonic
functions are constant:
\[
 0=\int_Nu\Delta u=\int_N|du|^2.
\]
Boundary-value problems require additional analytic hypotheses; the
representability theorem considered here concerns closed fibres.

Both source and target are sections of the trivial real line bundle
$N\times\R\to N$. The operator takes an entire section as its input
and returns another section.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Critical points of energies]
Let $N$ be a closed Riemannian manifold and let $V\colon\R\to\R$ be smooth. Consider
\[
 \mathcal E(u)=\int_N\left(\tfrac12|du|^2+V(u)\right)\,d\mathrm{vol}.
\]
Differentiating in the direction $v$ and integrating by parts gives
\[
 d\mathcal E_u(v)=\int_N\bigl(\Delta u+V'(u)\bigr)v\,d\mathrm{vol}.
\]
Therefore the critical points are the solutions of
$P(u)=0$, where
\[
 P\colon\Cinfty(N)\longrightarrow\Cinfty(N),\qquad
 P(u)=\Delta u+V'(u).
\]
The energy is a real-valued functional; its Euler--Lagrange operator $P$
has a function as its output. The metric and volume form identify the
first variation with this function.

Taking $V(u)=u^3/3$ gives the running example $P(u)=\Delta u+u^2$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Pseudo-holomorphic maps]
Let $(\Sigma,j)$ be a fixed Riemann surface and $(Z,J)$ an almost complex manifold.
A smooth map $u\colon\Sigma\to Z$ is pseudo-holomorphic when
\[
 \bar\partial_Ju=\tfrac12(du+J(u)\circ du\circ j)=0.
\]
The unknown is a section of $\Sigma\times Z\to\Sigma$.
Its output lies in
\[
 \Gamma\bigl(\Sigma;
 \operatorname{Hom}^{0,1}_{\R}(T\Sigma,u^*TZ)\bigr),
\]
where the superscript denotes complex-antilinear real linear maps.
The output bundle depends on $u$.

Globally, this is a section of a bundle over the space of maps.
Near a fixed map, exponential coordinates on $Z$ and identifications
of nearby output bundles turn it into an operator
\[
 Q\subseteq\Gamma(\Sigma;F)\longrightarrow\Gamma(\Sigma;E)
\]
for fixed finite-rank bundles $F,E$.
\Cref{chap:Elliptic_Equations_Kuranishi_Models} explains the coordinate principle, and \Cref{chap:Pseudo_Holomorphic_Curves_Derived_Moduli} carries out
the Cauchy--Riemann application. Keeping track of these source and target
bundles is necessary before applying an ellipticity theorem.
\end{example}

\section{Section stacks}

To apply the functor-of-points viewpoint, we retain smooth families of
sections and their pullbacks. We first fix the domain manifold, then
allow it to vary over a base.

We name bundles as in \citet[Section~3.4]{Steffens_Representability_Elliptic_Moduli}:
the unknowns of an equation are sections of submersions denoted $Y$,
and the equation takes values in sections of a submersion denoted $X$.

For a submersion $p\colon Y\to N$, the ordinary section set is
\[
 \Gamma(N;Y)=\{u\colon N\to Y:\ p\circ u=\operatorname{id}_N\}.
\]
If $Y$ is a vector bundle, this is a vector space.
A family parametrized by an ordinary manifold $T$ is a smooth map
$T\times N\to Y$ over $N$. Smoothness is joint smoothness in the
parameter and in the point of $N$.

The same formula makes sense when the parameter $T$ is a derived
manifold. It then uses maps of derived stacks, and so retains
infinitesimal and higher data in the parameter.

\begin{definition}[Section stack]
\label[definition]{def:Section_Stack}
The section stack of $Y\to N$ is the stack on derived manifolds given by
\[
 \Sec_N(Y)(T)=\Hom_{/N}(T\times N,Y).
\]
\end{definition}

This is the mapping stack often written $\operatorname{Map}_N(N,Y)$.
The notation $\Gamma(N;Y)$ will be reserved for ordinary smooth
sections, and, for a vector bundle on compact $N$, their Fr\'echet
manifold. Two computations show what the section stack records on
derived manifolds.

\begin{example}[Sections with coefficients]
\label[example]{ex:Sections_With_Coefficients}
Let $Y=N\times\R^n$ be a trivial bundle and let $T=\Spec A$ be a derived
manifold. Then $T\times N=\Spec(A\otimes_\infty\Cinfty(N))$, and a map
$T\times N\to\R^n$ is a map of animated $\Cinfty$-rings
$\Cinfty(\R^n)\to A\otimes_\infty\Cinfty(N)$. Since $\Cinfty(\R^n)$ is
free on $n$ generators,
\[
 \Sec_N(N\times\R^n)(\Spec A)\simeq\bigl(A\otimes_\infty\Cinfty(N)\bigr)^n,
\]
the $n$-fold product of the underlying anima. A family over $\Spec A$ is
thus an $n$-tuple of functions on $N$ with coefficients in $A$
\cite[proof of Lemma~3.2.11]{Steffens_Representability_Elliptic_Moduli}.
For $A=\Cinfty(T)$ with $T$ ordinary, this recovers the jointly smooth
families $\Cinfty(T\times N)^n$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Tangent vectors]
\label[example]{ex:Section_Stack_Tangent_Vectors}
Let $D=\Spec(\R[\varepsilon]/(\varepsilon^2))$. As in
\Cref{thm:Koszul_Presentation_Derived_Zero_Locus}, $D$ is the derived
zero locus of $t^2$ on $\R$. Indeed, multiplication by $t^2$ is
injective on $\Cinfty(\R)$, so the two-term Koszul complex has homology
only in degree zero, where it is $\Cinfty(\R)/(t^2)$.
Taking the product with $N$ gives the derived zero locus of $t^2$ on
$N\times\R$. The same calculation applies: a smooth function annihilated
by $t^2$ vanishes on the dense open $N\times(\R\setminus\{0\})$.
Thus the animated coproduct is discrete, and Taylor expansion in $t$
with smooth remainder gives
\[
 \R[\varepsilon]/(\varepsilon^2)\otimes_\infty\Cinfty(N)
 \simeq \Cinfty(N\times\R)/(t^2)
 \simeq \Cinfty(N)[\varepsilon]/(\varepsilon^2).
\]
The last map sends $[h]$ to $h(-,0)+\varepsilon\,\partial_t h(-,0)$.
The chain rule shows that it respects the smooth operations.

For the trivial bundle $N\times\R^n$, a family over $D$ is therefore
$u+\varepsilon v$ with $u,v\in\Cinfty(N)^n$. For a submersion
$Y\to N$, use submersion charts along a section $u$. Under a change of
chart the coefficient $v$ transforms by the derivative in the fibre
direction. Families restricting to $u$ at $\varepsilon=0$ therefore
identify the tangent space of $\Sec_N(Y)$ at $u$ with $\Gamma(N;u^*T_{Y/N})$.
\end{example}

To let the domain vary, we use families over a smooth stack.

\begin{definition}[Families over a smooth stack]
\label[definition]{def:Family_Of_Manifolds_Over_Stack}
Let $S$ be a smooth stack. A \emph{family of manifolds} over $S$ is a map
$q\colon M\to S$ of smooth stacks whose pullback along every map
$T\to S$ from an ordinary manifold is a submersion $M\times_ST\to T$ of
ordinary manifolds. It is \emph{proper} if each such pullback is proper.
A map $Y\to M$ over $S$ is a \emph{family of submersions} if
$M\to S$ is a family of manifolds and each pullback
$Y\times_ST\to M\times_ST$ is a submersion of ordinary manifolds.
\end{definition}

For a manifold base these are the usual submersions and proper
submersions. Steffens phrases these conditions using submersive maps of
smooth stacks \cite[Definition~2.1.60]{Steffens_Representability_Elliptic_Moduli}.
Suppose $q\colon M\to S$ is a family of manifolds and $Y\to M$ is a
family of submersions.

\begin{definition}[Relative section stack]
\label[definition]{def:Relative_Section_Stack}
The relative section stack $\Sec_{M/S}(Y)\to S$ is characterized by
\[
 \Hom_{/S}(T,\Sec_{M/S}(Y))
 \cong\Hom_{/M}(T\times_S M,Y).
\]
It is the Weil restriction $\operatorname{Res}_{M/S}(Y)$.
\end{definition}

The terminology describes the right adjoint to pullback along $M\to S$
\cite[Definition~3.2.1]{Steffens_Representability_Elliptic_Moduli}.
It exists because the $\infty$-category of stacks is an $\infty$-topos,
and hence locally cartesian closed.
The formula also proves base-change compatibility:
\[
 \Sec_{M/S}(Y)\times_S T
 \cong\Sec_{M_T/T}(Y_T),
 \qquad M_T=M\times_S T.
\]
Over $s\in S$, its fibre is the section stack on $M_s$.

\section{Solution stacks as derived fibres}

Let $P\colon\Sec_N(Y)\to\Sec_N(X)$ be any morphism of stacks, and let
$b$ be a section of $X$. Define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Solution_Stack_Inhomogeneous}
 \Sol(P;b)=\Sec_N(Y)\times_{\Sec_N(X)}^{\mathrm{der}}*,
\end{equation}
where the second map classifies $b$.
The adjective derived emphasizes the category in which the pullback is
taken; all pullbacks in derived stacks are already homotopy pullbacks.
As a limit of stacks, $\Sol(P;b)$ is again a stack.

Over a derived manifold $T$, a solution is a family $u$ together with a
homotopy from $P(u)$ to the constant family $b_T$.
Thus a solution stack can be defined without any finite-order assumption.
One does need a morphism of stacks, not merely a map between sets
of smooth sections.

When $Y$ and $X$ are vector bundles, a smooth map between the Fr\'echet
spaces $\Gamma(N;Y)$ and $\Gamma(N;X)$ supplies such a morphism.
A smooth map between open subsets gives a morphism between the
corresponding open substacks. By the exponential law, the smooth stack of
$\Gamma(N;Y)$ is the restriction of $\Sec_N(Y)$ to ordinary manifolds.
Conversely, the derived section stack is recovered from this restriction
by the functor $j_!$ of \Cref{sec:Smooth_Stacks_As_Derived_Stacks}
\cite[Lemma~3.2.11]{Steffens_Representability_Elliptic_Moduli}. As $j_!$
is fully faithful, maps of smooth stacks between section spaces extend
uniquely to maps of derived section stacks; see
\eqref{eq:Section_Stack_Convenient_Comparison} in
\Cref{chap:Elliptic_Representability_Smooth_Derived_Bridge}. This is a
genuine theorem rather than a formality: restriction to ordinary
manifolds can lose information, as the double point of
\Cref{sec:Smooth_Stacks_As_Derived_Stacks} shows. For example, on $S^1$
the map
\[
 u\longmapsto\left(x\longmapsto\int_{S^1}u\,d\theta\right)
\]
is a smooth linear map of section manifolds and defines a solution stack.
It is not a finite-order differential operator: changing $u$ away from
$x$ can change the output at $x$ while leaving every derivative there fixed.

For an operator $P\colon\Sec_{M/S}(Y)\to\Sec_{M/S}(X)$ over $S$, a
right-hand side is a section $b\colon S\to\Sec_{M/S}(X)$. The solution
stack over $S$ is the pullback
\[
 \Sol(P;b)=\Sec_{M/S}(Y)\times_{\Sec_{M/S}(X)}S
\]
along $b$.
All these constructions commute with base change.
The finite-order differential condition introduced next provides a
geometric way to define $P$ and the analytic structure needed for
representability.

\section{Jets and finite-order operators}

Jets encode the dependence of an operator on finitely many derivatives.
Their relative construction also makes the induced map of section
stacks available for families over derived manifolds.

A $k$-jet of a local section of $Y\to N$ at $x\in N$ records its value
and its derivatives through order $k$ at $x$.
Two sections define the same jet when those derivatives agree in local
coordinates. The chain rule makes this independent of the coordinates.
These jets form the bundle $J_N^k(Y)\to N$.

For example, for $Y=N\times\R$, a first jet records a scalar and a covector.
A second jet records the second derivatives as well; a connection
identifies that last datum with a symmetric tensor.
The jet prolongation sends a section $u$ to $j^ku$.

\begin{definition}[Finite-order differential operator]
\label[definition]{def:Finite_Order_Differential_Operator}
A nonlinear differential operator of order at most $k$ from $Y$ to $X$
over $N$ is a smooth map
\[
 p\colon J_N^k(Y)\longrightarrow X
\]
over $N$. Its induced map on sections is
$P(u)=p\circ j^ku$.
\end{definition}

Using the lowercase $p$ distinguishes the finite-dimensional map on
jets from the induced map $P$ on sections.
The latter is determined at $x$ by finitely many derivatives of $u$ at
that same point. A general map on sections need not have this locality.

For $\Delta u+u^2$, $k=2$. In local coordinates the Laplacian depends
on derivatives of $u$ of order at most two, while $u^2$ uses only its value.
For the Cauchy--Riemann equation only first jets are needed.

For $M\to S$, relative jets $J^k_{M/S}(Y)$ record derivatives in the
directions tangent to the fibres $M_s$. No differentiation in the
parameter $s$ is involved. The resulting map $P$ is therefore an
$S$-family of operators on the fibre manifolds.

The derived jet stack has a coordinate-independent construction.
Let $(M/S)^{(k)}$ be the order-$k$ infinitesimal neighbourhood of the
relative diagonal, with projections $\pi_1,\pi_2$ to $M$. Then
\[
 J^k_{M/S}(Y)
 =\operatorname{Res}_{(M/S)^{(k)}/M}
       \bigl(Y\times_{M,\pi_1}(M/S)^{(k)}\bigr),
\]
where the Weil restriction uses $\pi_2$.
It parametrizes sections on infinitesimal neighbourhoods.
For an ordinary submersion this recovers the classical jet bundle.
The construction commutes with base change along maps of smooth base
stacks \cite[Definition~3.3.7 and Proposition~3.3.9(2)]
{Steffens_Representability_Elliptic_Moduli}.

To prolong a family over a derived manifold $T\to S$, start with its
section $u\colon T\times_S M\to Y$ over $M$. Pull it back along
$\mathrm{id}_T\times\pi_1$ to obtain a section of $\pi_1^*Y$ over
$T\times_S(M/S)^{(k)}$. This is the pullback of the thickening along
$T\times_S M\to M$ when we use $\pi_2$ as its structure map.
The Weil restriction adjunction therefore gives
\[
 j^ku\colon T\times_S M\longrightarrow J^k_{M/S}(Y)
\]
over $M$. Thus $\pi_2$ specifies the point at which we take the jet,
and $\pi_1$ evaluates $u$ on its infinitesimal neighbourhood.
Composition with $p\colon J^k_{M/S}(Y)\to X$ gives $P(u)$, naturally
in $T$ \cite[end of Section~3.3]{Steffens_Representability_Elliptic_Moduli}.

\section{Differential moduli problems and ellipticity}

We now collect the geometric data of an equation into a differential
moduli problem. Its linearization gives the deformation-obstruction
complex, and its principal symbol determines whether elliptic analysis
can be applied.

A useful common formulation asks two operators to have the same output:
\[
 P_1(u_1)=P_2(u_2).
\]
It includes $P(u)=b$ by taking the second unknown bundle to be $N\to N$,
whose only section is the identity, and taking its operator to be $b$.

\begin{definition}
\label[definition]{def:Differential_Moduli_Problem}
Over a family $M\to S$, a differential moduli problem consists of
submersions $Y_1,Y_2,X\to M$ and maps
\[
 p_i\colon J^k_{M/S}(Y_i)\longrightarrow X.
\]
Its solution stack is
\[
 \Sol(\mathcal E)=
 \Sec_{M/S}(Y_1)
 \times_{\Sec_{M/S}(X)}
 \Sec_{M/S}(Y_2),
\]
with maps induced by $p_1,p_2$.
\end{definition}

This is \cite[Definitions~3.4.1--3.4.2]
{Steffens_Representability_Elliptic_Moduli}.
The order $k$ is a common order: an operator of lower order can be
composed with a jet truncation map. Ellipticity below is then a
condition on the symbol for that chosen common order.

When $S$ is a smooth stack, these data are specified on manifold
families over $S$ and are compatible with pullback. Relative jets and
section stacks commute with that pullback, so the equations and
solution stacks descend. This includes families with symmetries
encoded in the base stack.

Fix first $S=*$ and a solution $u$ of $P(u)=b$.
Variations of $u$ are sections of
$F=u^*T_{Y/N}$; variations of the output at $b$ are sections of
$E=b^*T_{X/N}$. Differentiating the finite jet formula gives a linear
differential operator
\[
 D_uP\colon\Gamma(N;F)\longrightarrow\Gamma(N;E).
\]
In coordinates it has the form
\[
 D_uP(v)=\sum_{|\alpha|\leq k}A_\alpha(x)\partial^\alpha v.
\]
Its coefficients depend on $u$ and its derivatives, but the expression
is linear in $v$.

The kernel of $D_uP$ is visible in the solution stack itself. By
\Cref{ex:Section_Stack_Tangent_Vectors}, a family over
$D=\Spec(\R[\varepsilon]/(\varepsilon^2))$ in $\Sol(P;b)$ restricting to
$u$ is a section $v$ of $F$ together with an identification
$P(u+\varepsilon v)\simeq b_D$, where $b_D$ is the constant family.
A map $D\times N\to X$ corresponds to a map of animated smooth rings
from $\Cinfty(X)$ into the discrete ring of functions on $D\times N$,
so its mapping anima is a set. The same holds for maps to $N$.
Taking the fibre over the projection $D\times N\to N$ shows that
$\Sec_N(X)(D)$ is a set as well. The identification with $b_D$
therefore amounts to the equation $D_uP(v)=0$.
Thus $\ker D_uP$ is the tangent space of $\Sol(P;b)$ at $u$.

For a matching problem and solution $(u_1,u_2)$ with common output $w$,
put
\[
 F=u_1^*T_{Y_1/N}\oplus u_2^*T_{Y_2/N},\qquad
 E=w^*T_{X/N}.
\]
The linearized matching equation is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Tangent_Complex_Solution_Stack}
 D(v_1,v_2)=D_{u_1}P_1(v_1)-D_{u_2}P_2(v_2).
\end{equation}
Its deformation-obstruction complex is
$[\Gamma(N;F)\xrightarrow D\Gamma(N;E)]$ in degrees $0,-1$.
Its degree-zero homology is the kernel, the space of infinitesimal
solutions. Its degree $-1$ homology is the cokernel, which receives
obstructions to solving inhomogeneous correction equations.
For an elliptic problem on a closed fibre, the representability proof
will show that this complex is
quasi-isomorphic to the finite-dimensional tangent complex of a local
derived chart
(\Cref{chap:Elliptic_Representability_Derived_Charts}).

Ellipticity is a condition on the highest-order part of the linearized
operator. In particular, it depends on the source and target bundles
of the full matching equation.

The highest-order part has a coordinate-free description through jets.
For a vector bundle $F\to N$ and $k\geq1$, the jet bundles fit into exact sequences
\[
 0\longrightarrow\operatorname{Sym}^k(T^*N)\otimes F
 \longrightarrow J_N^k(F)\longrightarrow J_N^{k-1}(F)
 \longrightarrow0.
\]
The kernel consists of the $k$-th derivatives of sections whose lower
derivatives vanish at the point. Connections can split these sequences,
but the sequences themselves are canonical. A linear differential
operator $D$ of order $k$ from $F$ to $E$ is given by a bundle map
$J_N^k(F)\to E$, and its restriction to
$\operatorname{Sym}^k(T^*N)\otimes F$ is the principal symbol.

\begin{definition}[Principal symbol and ellipticity]
\label[definition]{def:Elliptic_Differential_Moduli_Problem}
Let $D$ be a linear differential operator of order $k$ from $F$ to $E$.
Its principal symbol at a covector $\xi\in T^*_xN$ is the linear map
\[
 \sigma_D(x,\xi)\colon F_x\longrightarrow E_x,\qquad
 \sigma_D(x,\xi)(w)=D\bigl(\tfrac{1}{k!}f^k\tilde w\bigr)(x),
\]
where $f$ is a function with $f(x)=0$ and $d_xf=\xi$, and $\tilde w$ is
a section with $\tilde w(x)=w$. In local coordinates, if
$D=\sum_{|\alpha|\leq k}A_\alpha\partial^\alpha$, then
\[
 \sigma_D(x,\xi)=\sum_{|\alpha|=k}A_\alpha(x)\xi^\alpha.
\]
The operator is elliptic if $\sigma_D(x,\xi)$ is invertible for every
nonzero covector $\xi$.
A differential moduli problem is elliptic if its linearized matching
operator is elliptic at every solution on every fibre $M_s$.
\end{definition}

To verify the formula, choose coordinates $h$ centred at $x$ and a
local frame for $F$. The linear term of $f$ is $\xi(h)$ and the
constant term of $\tilde w$ is $w$. Hence the Taylor polynomial of
$f^k\tilde w/k!$ through degree $k$ is
\[
 \frac{\xi(h)^k}{k!}\,w
 =\sum_{|\alpha|=k}\frac{\xi^\alpha}{\alpha!}h^\alpha w.
\]
Its derivatives of order less than $k$ vanish at $x$, and its
$\alpha$-th derivative there is $\xi^\alpha w$ for $|\alpha|=k$.
Applying $D$ gives the stated coordinate formula and shows that the
symbol depends only on $\xi$ and $w$.

This real symbol convention omits the optional factors of $i$ used in
Fourier analysis; the invertibility condition is unchanged.
For the nonnegative Laplacian the real symbol with this convention is
$-|\xi|^2$, while the Fourier convention gives $|\xi|^2$.
Both are invertible away from the zero covector.

At a point of a positive-dimensional fibre $M_s$, we can choose a
nonzero covector $\xi$. Ellipticity then gives an isomorphism
$\sigma_D(x,\xi)\colon F_x\to E_x$. For a matching problem, the
fibre dimensions of $Y_1$ and $Y_2$ at the solution must therefore
add up to that of $X$ there. For $P(u)=b$, the second unknown bundle
is $N\to N$, whose fibres are points.

Ellipticity concerns the highest-order part. On a compact manifold
it leads to finite-dimensional kernel and cokernel after Sobolev
completion. It does not assert surjectivity. The latter failure is
exactly why a derived zero locus can be needed.

\section{Solutions and linearization of the running example}

Return to $P(u)=\Delta u+u^2$ on a closed connected manifold $N$.
If $P(u)=0$, integration gives
\[
 0=\int_N(\Delta u+u^2)=\int_Nu^2,
\]
so $u=0$. Its ordinary solution set is one point.

Differentiating gives
\[
 D_uP(v)=\Delta v+2uv.
\]
The zeroth-order term does not change the principal symbol, so this
operator is elliptic. At zero it is $\Delta$.
The standard solvability theorem for the Laplacian on a closed connected
manifold says that its image consists of the functions of integral zero.
Consequently,
\[
 \ker\Delta\cong\R,\qquad\operatorname{coker}\Delta\cong\R.
\]
Constants give representatives in both cases.

We can see an obstruction by trying to lift a first-order solution
$u=\varepsilon a$, with $a\in\R$ constant, from
$\R[\varepsilon]/(\varepsilon^2)$ to $\R[\varepsilon]/(\varepsilon^3)$.
A lift has the form $u=\varepsilon a+\varepsilon^2w$ for a smooth
function $w$ on $N$. The coefficient of $\varepsilon^2$ in $P(u)=0$
gives the correction equation
\[
 \Delta w+a^2=0.
\]
Integrating gives $a^2\operatorname{vol}(N)=0$. Thus every nonzero
first-order solution is obstructed at second order: its obstruction
is the class of the constant function $a^2$ in $\operatorname{coker}\Delta$.

\Cref{chap:Elliptic_Representability_Derived_Charts} will show that
$c^2=0$ is the full local Kuranishi equation. Near zero, the
derived solution stack is the double point
$\Spec(\R[c]/(c^2))$. As observed in
\Cref{sec:Smooth_Stacks_As_Derived_Stacks}, the double point and the
point take the same values on ordinary manifolds. On the dual numbers,
the maps $c\mapsto\varepsilon a$ recover exactly the first-order
solutions above.

\section{Further examples and exercises}

We illustrate parameter dependence with a family of metrics and give
two further calculations.

A smooth family of metrics $g_s$ gives operators
$P_s(u)=\Delta_{g_s}u+u^2$ on the fibres. For a family parametrized by
a derived manifold $T\to S$, the equation is evaluated using the
pulled-back metric.
The parameter affects the coefficients, while all derivatives of $u$
remain in the fibre directions.
The relative solution stack is defined before any assertion about
its projection to $S$ being a submersion or being representable.

\begin{exercise}
Compute the Euler--Lagrange operator for
$\int_N(\tfrac12|du|^2+\tfrac14u^4-\tfrac12u^2)$ and its linearization
at each constant solution. Check ellipticity.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
Let $P_1,P_2$ be first-order linear operators with a common target.
Write the principal symbol of the matching equation.
Explain why ellipticity of either operator individually does not
imply ellipticity of their difference on the direct sum of the sources.
\end{exercise}

The section and jet constructions are in
\cite[Sections~3.2--3.3 and Definitions~3.4.1--3.4.2]
{Steffens_Representability_Elliptic_Moduli}.
The remainder of the seminar proves Steffens's relative elliptic
representability theorem
\cite[Theorem~3.4.3]{Steffens_Representability_Elliptic_Moduli}: if
$M\to S$ is a proper family of manifolds and $\mathcal E$ is an elliptic
differential moduli problem over $M$, then $\Sol(\mathcal E)\to S$ is
representable by quasi-smooth derived $\Cinfty$-schemes locally of
finite presentation. \Cref{thm:Relative_Elliptic_Representability}
states it precisely and begins the proof.
