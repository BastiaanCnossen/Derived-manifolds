\chapter{Tangent and cotangent complexes}
\label[chapter]{chap:Stable_Linearization_And_Tangent_Complexes}

Let $x$ be a zero of a smooth map $f\colon U\to\R^r$. A first-order
variation $v\in T_xU$ preserves the equation precisely when $d_xf(v)=0$.
If the equation itself is varied by a vector $e\in\R^r$, the linearized
problem becomes $d_xf(v)=e$. Its solvability is measured by the class of
$e$ in $\operatorname{coker}(d_xf)$. Both questions are encoded by
\[
 [T_xU\xrightarrow{d_xf}\R^r]
\]
in degrees $0$ and $-1$.

The Koszul model of the preceding chapter lets us differentiate a chosen
list of equations. We want a construction that depends on the derived
zero locus itself. The cotangent complex supplies this intrinsic
linearization by representing smooth derivations.

We begin with ordinary differentials and explain why their relations
must be retained in a complex. Fibres and cofibres then provide the
linear algebra needed to define and calculate the cotangent complex.
Its base-change and transitivity properties lead back to the displayed
two-term tangent complex, whose homology describes infinitesimal
deformations and obstructions. We quote the existence and module
comparison theorems; the zero-locus calculation is the main conclusion.

\section{Smooth derivations and differentials}
\label[section]{sec:Smooth_Derivations}

Let $A$ be an ordinary $\Cinfty$-ring and let $M$ be an $A$-module.
A \emph{smooth derivation} is a map $D\colon A\to M$ satisfying
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Cinfty_Derivation_Chain_Rule}
 D\bigl(\varphi(a_1,\ldots,a_n)\bigr)
 =\sum_i(\partial_i\varphi)(a_1,\ldots,a_n)D(a_i)
\end{equation}
for every smooth function $\varphi\colon\R^n\to\R$. Addition,
multiplication, and constants among the smooth operations show that $D$
is $\R$-linear and satisfies the Leibniz rule. The displayed condition
also requires compatibility with the nonpolynomial smooth operations.

\begin{definition}[Smooth K\"ahler differentials]
\label[definition]{def:Smooth_Kahler_Differentials}
The module $\Omega_A^1$ of smooth K\"ahler differentials is equipped with
a derivation $d\colon A\to\Omega_A^1$ such that
\[
 \Hom_A(\Omega_A^1,M)\cong\operatorname{Der}_{\Cinfty}(A,M)
\]
naturally in $M$.
\end{definition}

One construction takes the free $A$-module on symbols $da$, for $a\in A$,
and imposes all instances of the smooth chain rule. The universal property
follows immediately from the imposed relations.

\begin{proposition}
\label[proposition]{prop:Differentials_Free_Cinfty_Ring}
For $A=\Cinfty(\R^n)$, there is an isomorphism
\[
 \Omega_A^1\cong\bigoplus_{i=1}^n A\,dx_i,\qquad
 dg=\sum_i\partial_i g\,dx_i.
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
A derivation is determined by its values $m_i=D(x_i)$, because the chain
rule forces $D(g)=\sum_i\partial_i g\,m_i$. Conversely, any such choice
defines a smooth derivation by the usual chain rule.
\end{proof}

For a manifold $U$, the corresponding formula is
\[
 \Omega_{\Cinfty(U)}^1\cong\Gamma(U,T^*U).
\]
It follows by localizing the coordinate calculation and gluing.
After tensoring with $\R$ along evaluation at $x$, its dual is $T_xU$.

For a quotient $A=B/I$, differentiating the equations gives a right exact
sequence
\[
 I/I^2\longrightarrow\Omega_B^1\otimes_B A
 \longrightarrow\Omega_A^1\longrightarrow0.
\]
Taking the cokernel discards the kernel of the first map. This matters
already for a hypersurface. For $B=\Cinfty(\R)$ and $I=(x^2)$, the
differentiated relation is $2x\,dx$; at the origin it vanishes. The ordinary
cotangent space has dimension one, but the equation still contributes
a relation direction. A complex can retain both.

The same issue appears in families. The maps $\R\xrightarrow{t}\R$ vary
smoothly with $t$, whereas both kernel and cokernel jump in dimension
at zero. Keeping the differential allows a single linear description
throughout the family.

\section{Complexes and the derived category}
\label[section]{sec:Complexes_And_Derived_Category}

To retain both solutions and relations, we work with complexes rather
than individual modules. We first describe their maps and homotopies,
then the shifts and cones that will be used to linearize a pullback.

A chain complex $C$ consists of real vector spaces and maps
\[
 \cdots\longrightarrow C_{n+1}\xrightarrow{d_{n+1}}C_n
 \xrightarrow{d_n}C_{n-1}\longrightarrow\cdots
\]
with $d_{n-1}d_n=0$. Its homology is
$H_n(C)=\ker(d_n)/\operatorname{im}(d_{n+1})$.
The underlying complexes of the preceding Koszul algebras are examples,
concentrated in nonnegative degrees. Here we allow all degrees.

A morphism of complexes commutes with the differentials. It is a
\emph{quasi-isomorphism} if it induces isomorphisms on all homology groups.
For example, the acyclic complex $[\R\xrightarrow{1}\R]$ is
quasi-isomorphic to zero. Adding this complex as a direct summand should
not change a linear invariant.

\begin{definition}[Derived $\infty$-category]
\label[definition]{def:Derived_Category_Real_Vector_Spaces}
The derived $\infty$-category $D(\R)$ is the $\infty$-categorical
localization of chain complexes of real vector spaces at
quasi-isomorphisms.
\end{definition}

Objects can be represented by complexes, and quasi-isomorphic complexes
represent isomorphic objects. Mapping objects are animae: they retain
homotopies between maps and their higher homotopies.

For vector spaces these can be calculated explicitly. A chain homotopy
from $f$ to $g$ is a degree $1$ map $h$ with $f-g=dh+hd$.
The mapping complex has terms
\[
 \underline{\Hom}(C,D)_n
 =\prod_p\Hom_\R(C_p,D_{p+n}),\qquad
 d(h)=d_Dh-(-1)^n h\,d_C.
\]
Its degree-zero cycles are chain maps, and its degree-zero homology
is chain maps modulo chain homotopy. Over a field this complex computes
the derived mapping object; positive homology gives the higher
homotopy groups of the mapping anima.

To move between degrees, we use shifts. The shift of $C$ is defined by
\[
 C[1]_n=C_{n-1},\qquad d_{C[1]}=-d_C.
\]
A vector space in degree zero moves to degree $1$ under $[1]$.
The inverse shift $[-1]$ moves it to degree $-1$.
Thus $H_n(C[m])=H_{n-m}(C)$.
In particular, a Koszul generator contributes in the degree of $A[1]$;
dualizing puts the corresponding tangent direction in degree $-1$.

As a simple example of higher homotopy, the mapping anima from $\R$
to $\R[1]$ has one component and fundamental group $\R$.
Every map is nullhomotopic, but its nullhomotopy is additional data.
Such data is part of a homotopy fibre.

Fibres and cofibers can be calculated using cones.
For a chain map $f\colon C\to D$, the cone is
\[
 \operatorname{Cone}(f)_n=D_n\oplus C_{n-1},\qquad
 d(y,x)=(d_Dy+f(x),-d_Cx).
\]
It represents the cofiber of $f$ in $D(\R)$.
Its shift $\operatorname{Cone}(f)[-1]$ represents the fibre.
These constructions replace cokernels and kernels by operations
invariant under quasi-isomorphism.

If $C,D$ are vector spaces in degree zero, the cone is
$[C\xrightarrow{f}D]$ in degrees $1,0$, so
\[
 H_1(\operatorname{Cone}(f))=\ker f,\qquad
 H_0(\operatorname{Cone}(f))=\operatorname{coker}f.
\]
For $f=0$, the cone is $D\oplus C[1]$. For an injective $f$, the cone
maps quasi-isomorphically to $\operatorname{coker}f$.
An ordinary short exact sequence
$0\to C\to D\to E\to0$ therefore gives a cofiber sequence $C\to D\to E$.

Every morphism fits into an exact triangle
\[
 C\xrightarrow{f}D\longrightarrow\operatorname{Cone}(f)
 \longrightarrow C[1].
\]
Its homology forms a long exact sequence
\[
 \cdots\longrightarrow H_n(C)\longrightarrow H_n(D)
 \longrightarrow H_n(\operatorname{Cone}(f))
 \longrightarrow H_{n-1}(C)\longrightarrow\cdots.
\]
The connecting map relates the neighbouring homology groups.
An exact triangle includes these maps and their compatibility, rather
than merely three objects.

Short exact sequences of complexes can have nonzero connecting maps even
over a field. Put $K=[\R\xrightarrow{1}\R]$ in degrees $1,0$.
Its degree-zero term is a subcomplex, giving a short exact sequence
\[
 0\longrightarrow\R\longrightarrow K\longrightarrow\R[1]
 \longrightarrow0.
\]
The middle complex is acyclic. In the derived category, its triangle
therefore becomes
\[
 \R\longrightarrow0\longrightarrow\R[1]\iso\R[1].
\]
The nonzero connecting map is essential to this triangle, even though
its middle object is zero.
This example also identifies the shift $\R[1]$ as the cofiber of
$\R\to0$.

The cone calculations describe categorical constructions in $D(\R)$.
The zero complex is both initial and terminal. The fibre and cofiber
of $f\colon A\to B$ fit into the squares
\[
 \begin{tikzcd}
 \operatorname{fib}(f)\rar\dar\drar[pullback]&A\dar["f"]\\
 0\rar&B
 \end{tikzcd}
 \qquad
 \begin{tikzcd}
 A\rar["f"]\dar\drar[pushout]&B\dar\\
 0\rar&\operatorname{cofib}(f).
 \end{tikzcd}
\]
Each square includes its specified commutativity homotopy. In $D(\R)$,
a square is a pullback if and only if it is a pushout. This can be
checked using the cone descriptions above. Consequently,
$A\to B\to C$, with a specified nullhomotopy of its composite, is
a fibre sequence exactly when it is a cofiber sequence.

The fibre and cofiber of the \emph{same map} differ by a shift:
\[
 \operatorname{cofib}(f)\cong\operatorname{fib}(f)[1].
\]
In particular, $A[1]=0\amalg_A0$ and $A[-1]=0\times_A0$.
For vector spaces concentrated in degree zero, a fibre-cofiber sequence
is precisely a short exact sequence. For general complexes, its
homology instead forms a long exact sequence, and no injectivity or
surjectivity of the first map is required. Finite products and
coproducts in $D(\R)$ agree and are given by direct sums of complexes.

\section{Modules and derived smooth derivations}
\label[section]{sec:Square_Zero_Extensions_And_Cotangent_Complexes}

We now apply this linear algebra to an animated smooth ring. Modules
provide the coefficients for infinitesimal variations, square-zero
extensions turn those variations into maps of smooth rings, and the
cotangent complex represents the resulting derivations.

The tensor product of real complexes gives $D(\R)$ a symmetric
monoidal structure with unit $\R$. Forgetting the nonpolynomial
smooth operations of an animated $\Cinfty$-ring $A$ gives a
connective commutative algebra object $A^{\mathrm{alg}}$ in $D(\R)$.
We define its modules in this $\infty$-category.

\begin{definition}
\label[definition]{def:Stable_Module_Animated_Cinfty_Ring}
The $\infty$-category $\operatorname{Mod}_A$ is the category of
modules over the commutative algebra object $A^{\mathrm{alg}}$ in
$D(\R)$.
\end{definition}

Informally, such a module is an object $M$ of $D(\R)$ equipped with
an action $A^{\mathrm{alg}}\otimes_\R M\to M$. Associativity and the
unit law are supplied by homotopies, together with their higher
compatibilities. Module morphisms likewise respect the action with
coherent homotopies, and the morphisms between two modules form an
anima. This is the higher-categorical notion of a module; the coherence
data is part of its structure.

For an explicit model, choose a $\Cinfty$-dg-algebra presenting $A$.
Then $\operatorname{Mod}_A$ is modeled by dg-modules over its underlying
commutative dg-algebra, with quasi-isomorphisms inverted in the
$\infty$-categorical sense. This description presents the module
category just defined. Modules may have homology in all degrees.
For an ordinary smooth ring, $\operatorname{Mod}_A$ is the derived
$\infty$-category of ordinary $A$-modules; in particular,
$\operatorname{Mod}_\R=D(\R)$.

Shifts, fibres, and cofibres work as they do for real complexes, and a
fibre sequence is also a cofiber sequence. In a dg-module model they
are calculated by shifts and cones of dg-modules.
Extension of scalars along $A\to B$ is written $M\otimes_A B$.
All tensor products over $A$ here are derived tensor products over
the underlying commutative algebras.

The smooth operations re-enter in square-zero extensions.
For ordinary $A,M$, the ring $A\oplus M$ has multiplication
\[
 (a,m)(b,n)=(ab,an+bm).
\]
The ideal $M$ has square zero. The smooth operations are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Smooth_Square_Zero_Formula}
 \varphi_{A\oplus M}((a_1,m_1),\ldots,(a_n,m_n))
 =\left(\varphi_A(a),\sum_i(\partial_i\varphi)_A(a)m_i\right).
\end{equation}
A section $a\mapsto(a,D(a))$ of $A\oplus M\to A$ preserves these
operations precisely when $D$ is a smooth derivation.

For animated $A$ and connective $M$, there is similarly a split square-zero
extension $A\oplus M$. In connective dg-models its underlying complex
is the direct sum, with the displayed multiplication and smooth operations
in degree zero. This construction respects the derived module category.
For a general module, $A\oplus M$ uses its connective cover,
since animated rings are connective. In our homological convention,
connectivity means that homology vanishes in negative degrees.

As in the ordinary case, we define the cotangent object by its maps
into every coefficient module. The sets of derivations are now replaced
by animae of derivations.

\begin{definition}[Derived derivations and the cotangent complex]
\label[definition]{def:Cotangent_Complex_Animated_Cinfty_Ring}
The anima of smooth derivations is
\[
 \operatorname{Der}_{\Cinfty}(A,M)
 =\Hom_{\operatorname{Alg}_{\Cinfty}(\An)_{/A}}(A,A\oplus M).
\]
The cotangent complex $\mathbb L_A$ represents this functor:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Cotangent_Complex_Universal_Property}
 \Hom_{\operatorname{Mod}_A}(\mathbb L_A,M)
 \cong\operatorname{Der}_{\Cinfty}(A,M).
\end{equation}
For $A\to B$, the relative cotangent complex $\mathbb L_{B/A}$
represents derivations of $B$ vanishing on $A$: the maps $B\to B\oplus M$
are taken over $B$ and under $A$.
\end{definition}

Existence is part of the general cotangent-complex construction.
The complex is connective, so maps from it depend only on the connective
cover of the target module, as required by the square-zero convention.
For an ordinary ring $A$, $H_0(\mathbb L_A)\cong\Omega_A^1$.
The other degrees retain relations in a presentation of $A$.
They may be present even when $A$ itself has no higher homotopy groups.

\begin{proposition}[Smooth manifolds]
\label[proposition]{prop:Cotangent_Complex_Manifold}
For a manifold $U$,
$\mathbb L_{\Cinfty(U)}\cong\Gamma(U,T^*U)$ in degree zero.
Its derived fibre at $x$ is $T_x^*U$.
\end{proposition}

For Euclidean space the proof uses universal derivations of a free
animated smooth ring. Smooth localization has vanishing relative
cotangent complex, so the calculation extends to coordinate opens
and then to manifolds. We use that localization result here; the
geometric construction appears in the next chapter.

\section{Base change and transitivity}
\label[section]{sec:Cotangent_Base_Change_Transitivity}

The universal definition makes the cotangent complex independent of a
presentation. The following two properties let us calculate it from one.
Base change transports equations, and transitivity compares successive
stages of imposing them.

\begin{theorem}[Base change]
\label[theorem]{thm:Cotangent_Complex_Base_Change}
For a pushout of animated smooth rings
\[
 \begin{tikzcd}
 A\rar\dar\drar[pushout]&B\dar\\
 A'\rar&B',
 \end{tikzcd}
\]
there is a natural isomorphism
$\mathbb L_{B/A}\otimes_B B'\cong\mathbb L_{B'/A'}$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}[Transitivity]
\label[theorem]{thm:Cotangent_Complex_Transitivity}
For $A\to B\to C$, there is a natural cofiber sequence
\[
 \mathbb L_{B/A}\otimes_B C\longrightarrow
 \mathbb L_{C/A}\longrightarrow\mathbb L_{C/B}.
\]
\end{theorem}

For base change, an $A'$-linear derivation of $B'$ is the same as an
$A$-linear derivation of $B$ with the indicated coefficients.
For transitivity, restriction gives a fibre sequence
\[
 \operatorname{Der}_B(C,M)\longrightarrow
 \operatorname{Der}_A(C,M)\longrightarrow
 \operatorname{Der}_A(B,M).
\]
The fibre includes the nullhomotopy expressing vanishing on $B$.
Representing these functors gives the cofiber sequence.
These arguments explain the formal content of the two theorems;
their construction for animated smooth rings is included in the
cotangent-complex theory cited below.

Absolute cotangent complexes are relative to the initial ring $\R$.
For $D=B\otimes_{\infty,A}^{\mathbb L}C$, base change and transitivity imply
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Algebraic_Pushout_Cotangent_Sequence}
 \mathbb L_A\otimes_A D
 \xrightarrow{(df,-dg)}
 (\mathbb L_B\otimes_B D)\oplus(\mathbb L_C\otimes_C D)
 \longrightarrow\mathbb L_D.
\end{equation}
This is a cofiber sequence. The signs identify the two copies of
a differential coming from $A$.

\section{The tangent complex of a zero locus}
\label[section]{sec:Linearizing_Derived_Pullbacks}

We can now recover the complex from the chapter opening without choosing
a dg-algebra resolution. We first linearize a pullback, specialize to a
vector bundle section, and interpret the two homology groups.

For a real point $x$ of a derived manifold $X$, write $\mathbb L_X|_x$
for the derived fibre of its cotangent complex.

\begin{definition}
\label[definition]{def:Tangent_Complex_At_A_Point}
The tangent complex is
$T_xX=\operatorname{RHom}_\R(\mathbb L_X|_x,\R)$.
\end{definition}

For $X=U\times_W V$ a derived pullback of ordinary manifolds,
\Cref{eq:Algebraic_Pushout_Cotangent_Sequence} at $(u,v)$ gives
\[
 T_w^*W\longrightarrow T_u^*U\oplus T_v^*V
 \longrightarrow\mathbb L_X|_{(u,v)}.
\]
Dualizing identifies
\[
 T_{(u,v)}X\cong
 [T_uU\oplus T_vV\xrightarrow{df-dg}T_wW]
\]
in degrees $0,-1$. Its degree-zero homology is the ordinary tangent
space of the equations.

Let $s$ be a section of $p\colon E\to U$, and let $s(x)=0_x$.
At the zero section there is a canonical splitting
\[
 T_{0_x}E\cong T_xU\oplus E_x.
\]
It uses $dp$, the derivative of the zero section, and the vertical tangent
of the vector bundle. Since $p\circ s$ is the identity,
$d_xs(v)=(v,D_xs(v))$ for a map $D_xs\colon T_xU\to E_x$.
This vertical linearization is canonical at a zero; no connection is needed.

The pullback of $s$ and the zero section has tangent complex
\[
 [T_xU\oplus T_xU\longrightarrow T_xU\oplus E_x],
 \qquad(v,w)\longmapsto(v-w,D_xs(v)).
\]
Changing source coordinates to $(v,v-w)$ separates the acyclic
identity complex on $T_xU$.

\begin{theorem}
\label[theorem]{thm:Tangent_Complex_Derived_Zero_Locus}
There are isomorphisms
\[
 T_xZ^{\mathrm{der}}(s)\cong[T_xU\xrightarrow{D_xs}E_x],\qquad
 \mathbb L_{Z^{\mathrm{der}}(s)}|_x
 \cong[E_x^\vee\xrightarrow{(D_xs)^\vee}T_x^*U],
\]
in degrees $0,-1$ and $1,0$, respectively.
\end{theorem}

The Koszul model gives the same answer. Its derived differentials
have generators $dx_j$ in degree zero and $d\varepsilon_i$ in degree $1$,
with differential
\[
 d_{\mathrm{complex}}(d\varepsilon_i)
 =d_{\mathrm{dR}}f_i
 =\sum_j\frac{\partial f_i}{\partial x_j}\,dx_j.
\]
Tensoring with the residue field gives the transpose of the Jacobian.
The relative free resolution used in the Koszul model is what makes
this a derived differential calculation.

The homology groups are
\[
 H_0(T_xZ^{\mathrm{der}}(s))=\ker D_xs,\qquad
 H_{-1}(T_xZ^{\mathrm{der}}(s))=\operatorname{coker}D_xs.
\]
The first consists of infinitesimal solutions. In coordinates,
$x+\varepsilon v$ over $\R[\varepsilon]/(\varepsilon^2)$ satisfies
the equation exactly when $D_xs(v)=0$.

The second is the target for obstruction classes. Perturbing the equation
by $\varepsilon e$ gives $D_xs(v)=-e$, whose obstruction is $[e]$
in the cokernel. Extending a first-order solution illustrates the same
phenomenon. In local coordinates and a bundle frame, write
\[
 x(\varepsilon)=x+\varepsilon v+\varepsilon^2w.
\]
Modulo $\varepsilon^3$, Taylor expansion gives
\[
 D_xs(v)=0,\qquad
 D_xs(w)+\tfrac12D_x^2s(v,v)=0.
\]
Thus the class of $\tfrac12D_x^2s(v,v)$ obstructs extension to second order.
The representative uses coordinates; the obstruction has an intrinsic
cotangent-complex interpretation. A nonzero obstruction space permits
nonzero obstruction classes, but does not imply that every deformation
is obstructed.

For $f(x)=x$ the tangent complex is acyclic. For $x^2$ at zero it is
$[\R\xrightarrow0\R]$: every $v$ solves the first-order equation,
but second-order extension requires $v^2=0$.
For $x^3$ the tangent complex is identical, and the first obstruction
occurs one order later. A tangent complex therefore does not determine
the nonlinear derived object.

For the zero section of $E$, the tangent complex is
$[T_xU\xrightarrow0 E_x]$. In all these cases,
\[
 \dim H_0(T_xX)-\dim H_{-1}(T_xX)=\dim U-\operatorname{rank}E.
\]
This is the virtual dimension. Its constancy is compatible with jumps
in the individual homology dimensions.

\section{Further reading}
\label[section]{sec:Supplement_Intrinsic_Stable_Modules}

The following observations distinguish smooth from algebraic
differentials and clarify how the finiteness of a tangent complex
differs from that of the homotopy of functions.

Modules have an algebraic description, while their extensions carry
smooth operations. Thus the smooth cotangent complex represents
smooth derivations. In general it cannot be replaced by the algebraic
cotangent complex of the underlying $\R$-algebra.
For example, $\Cinfty(\R)$ is free on one generator as a smooth ring,
but is not the polynomial $\R$-algebra on that generator.

A finitely presented animated smooth ring has a perfect cotangent complex.
After taking its fibre at a real point, a perfect complex is represented
by a bounded complex of finite-dimensional vector spaces. The tangent
complex at that point consequently has
finite-dimensional homology in finitely many degrees.
There is no universal two-term bound for all derived manifolds.
The two-term form above reflects the particular presentation by a
section of an ordinary vector bundle. The next chapter characterizes
the objects that have such local presentations.

For example, the free smooth dg-algebra $\R[z]$ with $|z|=2$ and
$dz=0$ is a finite cell algebra. Its derived differentials are freely
generated by $d_{\mathrm{dR}}z$ in degree $2$. At its real point,
the cotangent complex is $\R[2]$ and the tangent complex is $\R[-2]$,
concentrated in degree $-2$. Its function algebra has nonzero homotopy
in every nonnegative even degree. Bounded tangent information and
bounded homotopy of functions are therefore different finiteness conditions.

\begin{exercise}
For a linear map $f\colon V\to W$, write down its fibre complex and
verify that its cofiber is the shift of its fibre. Check zero maps
and identity maps.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
Calculate the tangent complexes of the derived zero loci of $(x,0)$
and $(x,x)$ as maps $\R\to\R^2$. Compare the Koszul models.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
For a smooth family of equations and zeros, show that the Euler
characteristic of the tangent complex is locally constant even where
its homology dimensions jump.
\end{exercise}
