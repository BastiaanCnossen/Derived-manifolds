\chapter{From ordinary to derived smooth objects}
\label[chapter]{chap:From_Smooth_Algebra_To_Derived_Manifolds}

\section{The ordinary finite-limit envelope}
\label[section]{sec:Ordinary_Finite_Limit_Envelope}

The preceding chapter established the structural theorem
\[
  \Cinfty(-)\colon
  \mathrm{Mfd}\catop
  \longrightarrow
  \Cinfty\text{-}\mathrm{Ring}^{\mathrm{fp}}.
\]
This functor is fully faithful, and it sends transverse pullback squares of
manifolds to pushout squares of $\Cinfty$-rings. Thus smooth algebra already
contains every manifold and every transverse intersection. It contains more:
the nontransverse line--parabola intersection produced the finitely presented
ring
\[
  \Cinfty(\R)/(x^2),
\]
which is not the function ring of a manifold.

The natural next question is not yet how to calculate a derived intersection.
It is a universal question.

\begin{question}
  \label[question]{question:Universal_Finite_Limit_Completion}
  What is the universal way to enlarge manifolds so that arbitrary finite
  pullbacks exist, while preserving the transverse pullbacks that are already
  correct?
\end{question}

There is already an answer if we ask this question only among ordinary
categories. Understanding that answer will show both why ordinary
$\Cinfty$-rings are useful and why they do not yet give derived manifolds.

\subsection{The algebraic theory of smooth functions}
\label[subsection]{sec:Algebraic_Theory_Smooth_Functions}

Let $\mathrm{Euc}$ denote the category whose objects are the Euclidean spaces
$\R^n$, for $n\geq0$, and whose morphisms are smooth maps. Its finite products
are the usual products
\[
  \R^n\times\R^m\cong\R^{n+m}.
\]
Talk 2 defined a $\Cinfty$-ring as a finite-product-preserving functor
\[
  A\colon\mathrm{Euc}\longrightarrow\mathrm{Set}.
\]
The underlying set of the ring is $A(\R)$. A smooth map
\[
  f\colon\R^n\longrightarrow\R^m
\]
induces an operation
\[
  A(f)\colon A(\R)^n\longrightarrow A(\R)^m.
\]

Nothing in this definition depends essentially on sets. It only uses finite
products.

\begin{definition}[$\Cinfty$-ring object]
  \label[definition]{def:Internal_Cinfty_Ring}
  Let $C$ be a category with finite products. A \emph{$\Cinfty$-ring object in
  $C$} is a finite-product-preserving functor
  \[
    R\colon\mathrm{Euc}\longrightarrow C.
  \]
  We write $\operatorname{Alg}_{\Cinfty}(C)$ for the category of
  $\Cinfty$-ring objects in $C$.
\end{definition}

The object $R(\R)$ carries an operation in $C$ for every smooth map between
Euclidean spaces. Since $R(\R^n)\cong R(\R)^n$, the functor is determined by
this one object together with its smooth operations. For $C=\mathrm{Set}$ we
recover ordinary $\Cinfty$-rings.

The definition is useful because a finite-limit-preserving functor transports
internal algebraic structure.

\begin{proposition}
  \label[proposition]{prop:Left_Exact_Transports_Cinfty_Rings}
  Let $F\colon C\to D$ preserve finite limits. If $R$ is a $\Cinfty$-ring
  object in $C$, then $F\circ R$ is a $\Cinfty$-ring object in $D$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
  A finite-limit-preserving functor preserves finite products. Therefore
  \[
    F(R(\R^n))
    \cong
    F(R(\R)^n)
    \cong
    F(R(\R))^n.
  \]
  The smooth operations and their identities are transported by functoriality.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Formal affine smooth objects}
\label[subsection]{sec:Formal_Affine_Smooth_Objects}

Set
\[
  \operatorname{Aff}_{\Cinfty}^{\mathrm{fp}}
  =
  (\Cinfty\text{-}\mathrm{Ring}^{\mathrm{fp}})\catop.
\]
For this chapter, an object of this opposite category will be called a
\emph{formal affine smooth object}. This is only a variance convention. We are
not constructing a topological space, a spectrum, or a structure sheaf.

There is a functor
\[
  j\colon\mathrm{Euc}
  \longrightarrow
  \operatorname{Aff}_{\Cinfty}^{\mathrm{fp}}
\]
which sends $\R^n$ to the free ring $\Cinfty(\R^n)$, regarded as an object of
the opposite category. A smooth map $f\colon\R^n\to\R^m$ induces
\[
  f^*\colon\Cinfty(\R^m)\longrightarrow\Cinfty(\R^n),
\]
which becomes a morphism in the same direction as $f$ after taking opposites.
The functor $j$ preserves finite products.

The category of formal affine smooth objects has the following universal
property.

\begin{theorem}[Ordinary finite-limit envelope]
  \label[theorem]{thm:Ordinary_Cinfty_Finite_Limit_Envelope}
  Let $C$ be an ordinary category with finite limits. Restriction along $j$
  induces an equivalence of categories
  \[
    \Fun^{\mathrm{lex}}
    \bigl(\operatorname{Aff}_{\Cinfty}^{\mathrm{fp}},C\bigr)
    \simeq
    \operatorname{Alg}_{\Cinfty}(C),
  \]
  where the left side consists of finite-limit-preserving functors.
\end{theorem}

This is the $0$-truncated case of the finite-limit-envelope theorem for an
algebraic theory
\cite[Theorem~3.22 and Remark~3.26]{Carchedi_Steffens_Universal_Derived_Manifolds}.
In the live route we state the theorem and unpack one finite presentation.
The presentation-independence and retract argument are retained here as the
proof architecture, but they are not a second live proof. Let us first unpack
what the theorem says.

A finitely presented $\Cinfty$-ring can be written
\[
  A=
  \frac{\Cinfty(\R^n)}{(f_1,\ldots,f_k)}.
\]
Equivalently, $A$ is the coequalizer
\[
  \Cinfty(\R^k)
  \mathrel{
    \substack{
      \xrightarrow{\ (f_1,\ldots,f_k)^*\ }\\[-.4ex]
      \xrightarrow[\ 0^*\ ]{}
    }
  }
  \Cinfty(\R^n)
  \longrightarrow A,
\]
where the $i$th coordinate function on $\R^k$ is sent respectively to $f_i$
and to zero. In the opposite category, this becomes an equalizer diagram
\[
  A
  \longrightarrow
  \Cinfty(\R^n)
  \mathrel{\substack{\longrightarrow\\[-.4ex]\longrightarrow}}
  \Cinfty(\R^k).
\]
Consequently, if $F$ preserves finite limits and
\[
  R=F\circ j\colon\mathrm{Euc}\longrightarrow C,
\]
then $F(A)$ is forced to be the internal zero locus
\[
  F(A)
  \cong
  \operatorname{Eq}
  \left(
    R(\R)^n
    \substack{\xrightarrow{(f_1,\ldots,f_k)}\\[-.6ex]
              \xrightarrow[0]{}}
    R(\R)^k
  \right).
\]
Thus the chosen $\Cinfty$-ring object determines the value of $F$ on every
finite presentation.

This argument explains uniqueness, but a theorem is still required. One must
show that the construction is independent of the chosen presentation,
functorial in $A$, and compatible with natural transformations. The general
proof observes that finitely presented algebras are generated from finitely
generated free algebras by finite colimits and retracts. Taking opposites
turns those operations into finite limits and retracts. This proof architecture
will return in the animated setting.

The theorem says that
\[
  \operatorname{Aff}_{\Cinfty}^{\mathrm{fp}}
\]
is not merely a convenient enlargement of Euclidean spaces. It is the
universal enlargement appropriate to ordinary finite-limit problems.

\section{What the ordinary envelope forgets}
\label[section]{sec:What_Ordinary_Envelope_Forgets}

The ordinary finite-limit envelope already computes zero loci. We now examine
exactly what it remembers.

\subsection{Zero loci as pullbacks}
\label[subsection]{sec:Ordinary_Formal_Zero_Loci}

Let $M$ be a manifold and let
\[
  f=(f_1,\ldots,f_k)\colon M\longrightarrow\R^k
\]
be smooth. The diagram of function rings is
\[
\begin{tikzcd}
  \Cinfty(\R^k) \rar["f^*"] \dar["0^*"'] \drar[pushout]
    & \Cinfty(M) \dar \\
  \R \rar & A_f.
\end{tikzcd}
\]
By the quotient formula from \Cref{prop:Cinfty_Coproduct_Formulas},
\[
  A_f
  \cong
  \frac{\Cinfty(M)}{(f_1,\ldots,f_k)}.
\]
After taking opposites, the corresponding formal affine object is the
pullback of
\[
  M\xrightarrow{f}\R^k\xleftarrow{0}*.
\]
This construction makes sense without a spectrum or a structure sheaf.

\begin{example}[A transverse zero]
  \label[example]{ex:Ordinary_Envelope_Transverse_Zero}
  For $f(x)=x$, we obtain
  \[
    \Cinfty(\R)/(x)\cong\R.
  \]
  The formal zero locus is the ordinary point, as required by the transverse
  theorem.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[A singular zero]
  \label[example]{ex:Ordinary_Envelope_Singular_Zero}
  For $f(x)=x^2$, we obtain
  \[
    \Cinfty(\R)/(x^2)
    \cong
    \R[\varepsilon]/(\varepsilon^2).
  \]
  The ordinary envelope therefore retains the nilpotent thickening found in
  Talk 3. Passing beyond manifolds did not yet require derived algebra.
\end{example}

These examples prevent one possible misconception. The existence of a
singular or nonreduced zero locus is not what distinguishes derived geometry
from ordinary smooth algebra.

\subsection{A self-intersection collapses}
\label[subsection]{sec:Ordinary_Self_Intersection_Collapses}

Let $A$ be a $\Cinfty$-ring, let $I\subseteq A$ be an ideal, and put
\[
  B=A/I.
\]
The self-intersection of the corresponding formal closed subobject is computed
by
\[
  B\amalg_A B.
\]

\begin{proposition}[Ordinary self-intersections]
  \label[proposition]{prop:Ordinary_Self_Intersections_Collapse}
  There is a natural isomorphism
  \[
    (A/I)\amalg_A(A/I)
    \cong
    A/I.
  \]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
  Apply the quotient pushout formula:
  \[
    (A/I)\amalg_A(A/I)
    \cong
    A/(I+I)
    =A/I.
  \]
\end{proof}

For example, let $A=\Cinfty(\R^2)$ and $I=(y)$. Then $B$ represents the
$x$-axis. Its ordinary self-intersection inside the plane is again represented
by $B$. The quotient remembers that $y$ vanishes, but it has no record that
the relation $y=0$ was imposed twice.

The conclusion must be stated carefully.

\begin{observation}
  \label[observation]{obs:Ordinary_Envelope_Is_Not_Wrong}
  The category $\operatorname{Aff}_{\Cinfty}^{\mathrm{fp}}$ is not failing its
  universal property. It is the correct finite-limit envelope among ordinary
  categories. The self-intersection calculation exposes a limitation of the
  ambient categorical language, not an error in the construction.
\end{observation}

An ordinary pullback is characterized by an isomorphism of sets
\[
  \Hom_C(T,X\times_ZY)
  \cong
  \Hom_C(T,X)
  \times_{\Hom_C(T,Z)}
  \Hom_C(T,Y).
\]
An element on the right is a pair of maps whose composites to $Z$ are equal.
In a set, equality is a property. Once the equality holds, there is no further
datum recording how it holds. In particular, imposing the same relation twice
cannot create new equality data.

This identifies the boundary of the ordinary construction:

\[
  \text{ordinary finite limits retain solutions and quotient relations,}
\]
but
\[
  \text{they cannot retain higher structure among those relations.}
\]

\section{Equality as structure}
\label[section]{sec:Equality_As_Structure}

We now introduce only the part of higher category theory needed to formulate a
better universal problem. The guiding principle is:

\begin{quote}
  When expressing that two entities are equal, specify how they are equal.
\end{quote}

This principle changes the primitive notion of a collection.

\subsection{From sets to animae}
\label[subsection]{sec:From_Sets_To_Animae}

\begin{definition}[Anima]
  \label[definition]{def:Anima_Conceptual}
  An \emph{anima} is a collection in which equality between elements is
  structure rather than a property. For elements $x,y$ of an anima $X$, the
  equalities from $x$ to $y$ form an anima $X(x,y)$. Equalities between such
  equalities again form animae, and this continues coherently.
\end{definition}

Every set determines an anima in which each equality anima is either empty or
contractible. These are the $0$-truncated animae. A topological space also has
an underlying anima: points are elements, paths are equalities, homotopies are
equalities between equalities, and so on.

We write $\An$ for the $\infty$-category of animae. The notation is meant to
emphasize that animae play the foundational role that sets play in ordinary
category theory. No particular model for animae will be used.

The corresponding replacement for an ordinary category is an
$\infty$-category. Its morphisms from $X$ to $Y$ form an anima
\[
  \Hom_C(X,Y)
\]
rather than merely a set. Composition preserves all the equality data. We
will use the ordinary categorical operations of products, pullbacks, functor
categories, and equivalences in this setting without choosing a model.

\subsection{A self-pullback remembers self-equalities}
\label[subsection]{sec:Self_Pullback_Remembers_Equalities}

Let $X$ be an anima and let $x\colon *\to X$ be a point. Form the pullback
\[
\begin{tikzcd}
  *\times_X* \rar \dar \drar[pullback]
    & * \dar["x"] \\
  * \rar["x"'] & X.
\end{tikzcd}
\]
An element of this pullback consists of the two chosen points together with an
equality between their images in $X$. Since both images are $x$, we obtain an
equivalence
\[
  *\times_X*
  \simeq
  X(x,x).
\]

In sets, the right side is a singleton. In animae, it can be highly
nontrivial. If
\[
  X=\Pi_\infty(S^1)
\]
is the underlying anima of the circle and $x$ is its base point, then
\[
  X(x,x)
  \simeq
  \Omega\Pi_\infty(S^1)
  \simeq
  \Z.
\]
The integers record the winding numbers of loops.

This example is the categorical prototype of a derived self-intersection. It
does not yet calculate the self-intersection of a smooth submanifold. It shows
what becomes possible once a pullback is allowed to retain the ways in which
two maps agree.

\subsection{Pullbacks in an \texorpdfstring{$\infty$}{infinity}-category}
\label[subsection]{sec:Pullbacks_In_An_Infinity_Category}

Let
\[
  X\xrightarrow{f}Z\xleftarrow{g}Y
\]
be a diagram in an $\infty$-category $C$. Its pullback is characterized by
natural equivalences of animae
\[
  \Hom_C(T,X\times_ZY)
  \simeq
  \Hom_C(T,X)
  \times_{\Hom_C(T,Z)}
  \Hom_C(T,Y)
\]
for all $T\in C$.

Informally, a generalized $T$-point consists of
\[
  a\colon T\to X,
  \qquad
  b\colon T\to Y,
  \qquad
  \alpha\colon f\circ a\cong g\circ b.
\]
The equality $\alpha$ is part of the datum. Equalities between choices of
$\alpha$, and all subsequent coherence, are contained in the pullback of hom
animae.

This is the only pullback notion needed in the sequel. In older sources the
phrase \emph{homotopy pullback} is often used to distinguish a construction in
a chosen presentation from an ordinary strict pullback. We will work
synthetically and simply say \emph{pullback} in an $\infty$-category.

The passage from hom sets to hom animae now gives a precise reason to reconsider
\Cref{question:Universal_Finite_Limit_Completion}. We should ask for a
universal finite-limit envelope among $\infty$-categories, not merely among
ordinary categories.

\section{The animated finite-limit envelope}
\label[section]{sec:Animated_Finite_Limit_Envelope}

The algebraic theory of smooth functions can be interpreted in animae just as
it was interpreted in sets.

\begin{definition}[Animated $\Cinfty$-ring]
  \label[definition]{def:Animated_Cinfty_Ring}
  An \emph{animated $\Cinfty$-ring} is a finite-product-preserving functor
  \[
    A\colon\mathrm{Euc}\longrightarrow\An.
  \]
  We denote their $\infty$-category by
  \[
    \operatorname{Alg}_{\Cinfty}(\An)
    =
    \Fun^\times(\mathrm{Euc},\An).
  \]
\end{definition}

An ordinary $\Cinfty$-ring is the special case in which every value $A(\R^n)$
is a set, regarded as a $0$-truncated anima. Animated rings allow the smooth
operations themselves to interact with higher equality data.

We need a homotopical version of finite presentation.

\begin{definition}[Homotopical finite presentation]
  \label[definition]{def:Homotopically_Finitely_Presented_Cinfty_Ring}
  An animated $\Cinfty$-ring $A$ is \emph{homotopically finitely presented} if
  it is compact in $\operatorname{Alg}_{\Cinfty}(\An)$: the functor
  \[
    \Hom_{\operatorname{Alg}_{\Cinfty}(\An)}(A,-)
    \colon
    \operatorname{Alg}_{\Cinfty}(\An)
    \longrightarrow
    \An
  \]
  preserves filtered colimits.
\end{definition}

We use the subscript $\mathrm{fp}$ for the full subcategory of such objects.
The word \emph{homotopically} matters: finite presentation is now a statement
about the entire hom anima, not only its set of connected components.

The central theorem applies to every algebraic theory. We state only its
$\Cinfty$ instance.

\begin{theorem}[Animated finite-limit envelope]
  \label[theorem]{thm:Animated_Cinfty_Finite_Limit_Envelope}
  Let $C$ be an idempotent-complete $\infty$-category with finite limits.
  Restriction along the finitely generated free animated $\Cinfty$-rings
  induces an equivalence of $\infty$-categories
  \[
    \Fun^{\mathrm{lex}}
    \left(
      \operatorname{Alg}_{\Cinfty}(\An)_{\mathrm{fp}}\catop,
      C
    \right)
    \simeq
    \operatorname{Alg}_{\Cinfty}(C).
  \]
\end{theorem}

This is Carchedi--Steffens, Theorem~3.22
\cite[Theorem~3.22]{Carchedi_Steffens_Universal_Derived_Manifolds}.
The ordinary \Cref{thm:Ordinary_Cinfty_Finite_Limit_Envelope} is its
$0$-truncated counterpart.

Recall that an $\infty$-category is idempotent-complete if every retract
diagram which exists formally is represented by an actual object. Ordinary
categories with finite limits are automatically idempotent-complete. This is
not automatic for $\infty$-categories, which is why the condition appears in
the theorem.

\subsection{Why the theorem is plausible}
\label[subsection]{sec:Why_Animated_Envelope_Theorem_Plausible}

This subsection is supplementary to the default live route. The talk uses the
theorem through its universal property; the compact-generation argument is
included for readers who want to know why the statement has this form.

The proof has four load-bearing steps.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item The free $\Cinfty$-ring on $n$ generators is compact. Mapping out of
    it is evaluation on an $n$-tuple, and evaluation commutes with filtered
    colimits.
  \item Every animated $\Cinfty$-ring is assembled as a colimit of free
    rings. This is the homotopical form of presenting an algebra by generators
    and relations.
  \item A compact animated $\Cinfty$-ring is a retract of a finite colimit of
    finitely generated free rings.
  \item After taking opposites, the preceding statement says that every object
    of
    $\operatorname{Alg}_{\Cinfty}(\An)_{\mathrm{fp}}\catop$
    is a retract of a finite limit of Euclidean objects. A
    finite-product-preserving functor on $\mathrm{Euc}$ therefore has a unique
    left exact extension.
\end{enumerate}

The complete argument constructs the extension by right Kan extension and
checks that it takes values in the chosen target $C$. The compactness and
idempotent-completeness hypotheses are precisely what make that last step
work. The important point for this seminar is the architecture:
\[
  \text{free algebras}
  \longrightarrow
  \text{finite colimits and retracts}
  \longrightarrow
  \text{finite limits after taking opposites}.
\]

\subsection{Comparison with the ordinary envelope}
\label[subsection]{sec:Compare_Ordinary_Animated_Envelopes}

The two envelope theorems have the same form:
\[
\begin{array}{c|c}
\text{ordinary} & \text{animated}\\
\hline
\mathrm{Set} & \An\\
\Cinfty\text{-rings} & \text{animated }\Cinfty\text{-rings}\\
(\Cinfty\text{-}\mathrm{Ring}^{\mathrm{fp}})\catop
& \operatorname{Alg}_{\Cinfty}(\An)_{\mathrm{fp}}\catop\\
\text{finite limits in ordinary categories}
& \text{finite limits in }\infty\text{-categories}.
\end{array}
\]

This comparison gives the conceptual transition. Derived smooth geometry is
not obtained by adding an arbitrary decoration to an ordinary zero locus. It
arises by asking the same universal algebraic question in a setting where
equalities and their coherences remain visible.

There is one technical warning.

\begin{warning}
  \label[warning]{warn:Ordinary_Fp_Not_Necessarily_Animated_Fp}
  An ordinary finitely presented $\Cinfty$-ring need not remain compact when it
  is regarded as a discrete animated $\Cinfty$-ring. Thus the ordinary
  envelope is not simply a full subcategory of the animated envelope obtained
  by declaring every ordinary object discrete.
\end{warning}

Carchedi and Steffens record the analogous warning for an arbitrary algebraic
theory
\cite[Warning~3.28]{Carchedi_Steffens_Universal_Derived_Manifolds}.
The truncation functor $\pi_0$ does send homotopically finitely presented
animated rings to finitely presented ordinary rings, but the reverse passage
can require retaining higher relation data.

In particular, the animated pushout associated with a self-intersection need
not collapse to the ordinary quotient. The next chapter will calculate this
phenomenon. No such calculation is needed to formulate the universal category.

\section{The universal property of derived manifolds}
\label[section]{sec:Universal_Property_Derived_Manifolds}

We can now return to the category of manifolds rather than only its Euclidean
subcategory.

\begin{definition}[Universal $\infty$-category of derived manifolds]
  \label[definition]{def:Universal_Infinity_Category_Derived_Manifolds}
  The $\infty$-category $\mathrm{DMfd}$ of \emph{derived manifolds} is equipped
  with a functor
  \[
    i\colon\mathrm{Mfd}\longrightarrow\mathrm{DMfd}
  \]
  and is characterized by the following properties.
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item The $\infty$-category $\mathrm{DMfd}$ has finite limits and is
      idempotent-complete.
    \item The functor $i$ preserves transverse pullbacks and the terminal
      object.
    \item For every idempotent-complete $\infty$-category $C$ with finite
      limits, restriction along $i$ induces an equivalence
      \[
        \Fun^{\mathrm{lex}}(\mathrm{DMfd},C)
        \simeq
        \Fun^{\pitchfork}(\mathrm{Mfd},C),
      \]
      where the right side consists of functors preserving transverse
      pullbacks and the terminal object.
  \end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

This is Carchedi--Steffens, Definition~2.1
\cite[Definition~2.1]{Carchedi_Steffens_Universal_Derived_Manifolds}.
As usual, the universal property determines $\mathrm{DMfd}$ uniquely. It also
makes the intended division of labor transparent:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Differential topology determines which existing pullbacks must remain
    unchanged, namely the transverse ones.
  \item Finite-limit completion adjoins the missing intersections.
  \item $\infty$-categorical universality retains all equality and coherence
    data needed to compare and iterate those intersections.
  \item Idempotent completion adds the retracts forced by the universal
    problem.
\end{enumerate}

The definition does not say what a derived manifold looks like in coordinates.
That is a feature at this stage. It separates the intrinsic theory from any
particular construction.

\subsection{From Euclidean operations to manifolds}
\label[subsection]{sec:From_Euclidean_Operations_To_Manifolds}

The connection between \Cref{def:Universal_Infinity_Category_Derived_Manifolds}
and $\Cinfty$-rings requires an additional geometric theorem. It does not
follow merely from the result of Talk 3.

This extension theorem is quoted in the live talk. Its role is essential, but
the right Kan extension and independence-of-presentation proof are not part of
the 70-minute route.

Let
\[
  q\colon\mathrm{Euc}\longrightarrow\mathrm{Mfd}
\]
be the inclusion. Carchedi and Steffens prove that, for every
idempotent-complete $\infty$-category $C$ with finite limits, restriction along
$q$ induces an equivalence
\[
  \Fun^{\pitchfork}(\mathrm{Mfd},C)
  \simeq
  \Fun^\times(\mathrm{Euc},C)
  =
  \operatorname{Alg}_{\Cinfty}(C).
\]
See Lemma~5.1 and Corollary~5.2 of their paper
\cite[Lemma~5.1 and Corollary~5.2]{Carchedi_Steffens_Universal_Derived_Manifolds}.

Here is the geometric reason. A product-preserving functor on Euclidean spaces
already knows how to interpret every smooth operation. Every manifold can be
exhibited as a retract of a transverse pullback built from Euclidean
manifolds. Since the target has finite limits and retracts, the functor extends
to that presentation. Preservation of transverse pullbacks makes the extension
independent of the geometric presentation. The proof organizes this extension
as a right Kan extension.

This theorem upgrades the result of Talk 3. Talk 3 proved that the actual
function-ring functor has the required compatibility. The extension theorem
says that this compatibility is universal: any internal $\Cinfty$-ring
determines, uniquely, a transverse-pullback-preserving functor on all
manifolds.

\subsection{The two universal properties agree}
\label[subsection]{sec:Two_Universal_Properties_Agree}

Combining the geometric extension theorem with
\Cref{def:Universal_Infinity_Category_Derived_Manifolds} gives the main result
of the chapter.

\begin{theorem}[Universal property through $\Cinfty$-rings]
  \label[theorem]{thm:Universal_Property_Derived_Manifolds_Cinfty_Rings}
  For every idempotent-complete $\infty$-category $C$ with finite limits,
  there is a natural equivalence
  \[
    \Fun^{\mathrm{lex}}(\mathrm{DMfd},C)
    \simeq
    \operatorname{Alg}_{\Cinfty}(C).
  \]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  By the universal property under manifolds,
  \[
    \Fun^{\mathrm{lex}}(\mathrm{DMfd},C)
    \simeq
    \Fun^{\pitchfork}(\mathrm{Mfd},C).
  \]
  Restriction to Euclidean spaces gives
  \[
    \Fun^{\pitchfork}(\mathrm{Mfd},C)
    \simeq
    \Fun^\times(\mathrm{Euc},C)
    =
    \operatorname{Alg}_{\Cinfty}(C).
  \]
  Composing these equivalences proves the claim.
\end{proof}

This is Carchedi--Steffens, Theorem~5.3
\cite[Theorem~5.3]{Carchedi_Steffens_Universal_Derived_Manifolds}.

The animated finite-limit envelope has exactly the same universal property.
We therefore obtain the promised algebraic presentation.

\begin{corollary}[Derived manifolds as formal animated affines]
  \label[corollary]{cor:Derived_Manifolds_Animated_Cinfty_Rings}
  There is an equivalence of $\infty$-categories
  \[
    \mathrm{DMfd}
    \simeq
    \operatorname{Alg}_{\Cinfty}(\An)_{\mathrm{fp}}\catop.
  \]
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
  By
  \Cref{thm:Animated_Cinfty_Finite_Limit_Envelope,thm:Universal_Property_Derived_Manifolds_Cinfty_Rings},
  both sides are idempotent-complete $\infty$-categories with finite limits
  which universally carry a $\Cinfty$-ring object. Universal objects are
  unique, so the two $\infty$-categories are equivalent.
\end{proof}

This is Carchedi--Steffens, Corollary~5.4
\cite[Corollary~5.4]{Carchedi_Steffens_Universal_Derived_Manifolds}.
It is important to keep the logical order straight:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item The universal property characterizes derived manifolds intrinsically.
  \item The corollary identifies that intrinsic $\infty$-category with an
    algebraic presentation.
  \item A particular computational model for animated $\Cinfty$-rings is not
    part of the definition.
\end{enumerate}

There is also a terminological boundary to keep visible. The symbol
$\mathrm{DMfd}$ here denotes the Carchedi--Steffens universal finite-limit
envelope. The derived $\Cinfty$-schemes appearing in the later elliptic
representability theorem belong to a spectrum-and-structure-sheaf formalism.
Talk 7 will construct the bridge from animated affine algebra to locally
affine derived smooth geometry. At this stage, we should not silently use the
two descriptions as definitions of one another.

\subsection{Consequences and the next question}
\label[subsection]{sec:Universal_Property_Consequences_Next_Question}

The universal property and its algebraic presentation have several immediate
consequences.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Manifolds embed fully faithfully into $\mathrm{DMfd}$.
  \item The $\infty$-category $\mathrm{DMfd}$ has arbitrary finite pullbacks.
  \item A transverse pullback of manifolds, regarded in $\mathrm{DMfd}$,
    agrees with the ordinary manifold pullback.
  \item A nontransverse diagram has a pullback in $\mathrm{DMfd}$ even when no
    ordinary manifold pullback exists.
  \item The construction is model-independent: any model satisfying the
    universal property presents the same $\infty$-category.
\end{enumerate}

The chapter has deliberately not calculated any of the new objects. We know
that the self-intersection of the $x$-axis exists in $\mathrm{DMfd}$, and we
know why its pullback can retain more than an ordinary quotient. We have not
yet identified the retained algebraic information.

That gives the next question:

\begin{question}
  \label[question]{question:Calculate_New_Derived_Pullbacks}
  What do the newly adjoined pullbacks look like in examples, and how can their
  infinitesimal information be calculated?
\end{question}

The next chapter will answer this for derived zero loci. Only after the
derived objects themselves are concrete will we introduce their tangent
complexes.

\section{Summary and supplementary materials}
\label[section]{sec:From_Smooth_Algebra_Summary_Supplementary_Materials}

\subsection{The live route}
\label[subsection]{sec:From_Ordinary_To_Derived_Live_Route}

The default live route occupies 70 minutes. The collapsing
self-intersection is the continuing example. It first exposes the limit of
the ordinary finite-limit envelope, then motivates equality as structure and
the passage from sets to animae. The ordinary and animated envelope theorems
are stated and interpreted rather than proved in full. The geometric
extension theorem is also quoted. Their common universal property yields the
algebraic presentation of derived manifolds and the handoff to the explicit
calculations of Talk 5.

\subsection{Takeaways}
\label[subsection]{sec:From_Smooth_Algebra_Takeaways}

The live route can be compressed into seven statements.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item A $\Cinfty$-ring object in a category $C$ is a
    finite-product-preserving functor $\mathrm{Euc}\to C$.
  \item The opposite category of finitely presented ordinary $\Cinfty$-rings
    is the universal finite-limit envelope appropriate to ordinary
    categories.
  \item This envelope contains ordinary singular zero loci but collapses the
    repeated relation in a self-intersection.
  \item Animae retain equality data, so a pullback can remember the ways in
    which two composites agree.
  \item Animated $\Cinfty$-rings give the corresponding finite-limit envelope
    among idempotent-complete $\infty$-categories.
  \item The universal property under manifolds is equivalent to the universal
    property of carrying a $\Cinfty$-ring object.
  \item Consequently,
    \[
      \mathrm{DMfd}
      \simeq
      \operatorname{Alg}_{\Cinfty}(\An)_{\mathrm{fp}}\catop.
    \]
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Supplement and related literature}
\label[subsection]{sec:Finite_Limit_Envelope_Supplement}

This final subsection records technical qualifications and references. It is not
part of the default live route.

\subsubsection{Finite presentation in the two envelopes}
\label[subsubsection]{sec:Finite_Presentation_Two_Envelopes}

In the ordinary category of $\Cinfty$-rings, finite presentation can be
expressed by finitely many generators and finitely many relations. It is also
equivalent to compactness with respect to filtered colimits.

In the $\infty$-category of animated $\Cinfty$-rings, compactness is stronger
than asking that $\pi_0A$ be finitely presented. It controls maps from $A$ into
filtered colimits as animae, including all higher homotopy groups of those hom
animae. This is why an ordinary finite presentation need not define a compact
object after discrete inclusion.

The useful structural statement is
\[
  \operatorname{Alg}_{\Cinfty}(\An)_{\mathrm{fp}}
  =
  \left\langle
    \text{finitely generated free animated $\Cinfty$-rings}
  \right\rangle_{\text{finite colimits and retracts}}.
\]
It is this statement, rather than a chosen resolution theory, which drives the
finite-limit-envelope theorem.

\subsubsection{Why idempotent completion appears}
\label[subsubsection]{sec:Why_Idempotent_Completion_Appears}

Informally, an idempotent looks like a morphism $e\colon X\to X$ together with
a homotopy from $e\circ e$ to $e$. Formally, an idempotent in an
$\infty$-category is a coherent idempotent diagram: the displayed homotopy is
part of the data, together with its higher compatibilities. Splitting it means
finding an object $Y$ and maps
\[
  Y\xrightarrow{i}X\xrightarrow{r}Y
\]
together with coherent identifications of $r\circ i$ with
$\operatorname{id}_Y$ and of $i\circ r$ with the given idempotent. In an
ordinary category with finite limits, idempotents split. For $\infty$-categories
this is not forced by finite limits alone. The compact algebraic objects are
closed under retracts, so their opposite category is naturally
idempotent-complete. The universal property of derived manifolds must record
this closure explicitly.

\subsubsection{Related literature}
\label[subsubsection]{sec:Finite_Limit_Envelope_Related_Literature}

The primary source is Carchedi--Steffens, \emph{On the universal property of
derived manifolds}. The universal property under manifolds is Definition~2.1,
the finite-limit-envelope theorem for an algebraic theory is Theorem~3.22, and
its ordinary counterpart is discussed in Remark~3.26
\cite[Definition~2.1, Theorem~3.22, and Remark~3.26]{Carchedi_Steffens_Universal_Derived_Manifolds}.
The comparison between functors on Euclidean spaces and functors on manifolds
is Lemma~5.1 and Corollary~5.2. The equivalence of the two universal properties
and the algebraic presentation of derived manifolds are Theorem~5.3 and
Corollary~5.4
\cite[Lemma~5.1, Corollary~5.2, Theorem~5.3, and Corollary~5.4]{Carchedi_Steffens_Universal_Derived_Manifolds}.

Spivak's axioms for derived smooth manifolds supply the earlier geometric
requirement that transverse pullbacks remain unchanged
\cite[Sections~1--3]{Spivak_Derived_Smooth_Manifolds}.
Carchedi--Steffens show that an extension of Spivak's construction satisfies
their universal property
\cite[Theorem~5.10]{Carchedi_Steffens_Universal_Derived_Manifolds}.

The spectrum and structure-sheaf theory of ordinary and derived
$\Cinfty$-schemes has been deliberately postponed to Talk 7. It gives a
geometric realization of affine $\Cinfty$-algebra and supplies the local
language needed for later descent and representability arguments, but it is
not required for the universal passage from Talks 2--3 to derived manifolds.
